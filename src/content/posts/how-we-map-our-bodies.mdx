In the eyes of Western medicine, health is the absence of disease, and disease is something wrong that needs to be removed. You get sick, go to the doctor for a pill that takes away the pain, a drug that pushes your allergies aside, a surgery that cuts out the tumor. You go to the doctor the way you would take a broken car to the mechanic — hand it over, let them find the broken part, fix it, put it back. You hand your body to the person in the white coat and say: "Fix me."

The doctor examines you, identifies the symptoms, diagnoses you, and prescribes you medicine. You take the medicine. When you get better, you praise the doctor and the pills.

Since when did we forget about the innate power of our bodies to heal, and our individual responsibility to nourish it?

## The Western map

What if the modern map of the body — the anatomy of organs, the glossary of parts — is not the only one that exists? This map was drawn largely during the scientific revolution of the 17th century, when thinkers like Descartes introduced reductive analysis, Newton described the cause-and-effect laws of the universe, and Pasteur's germ theory identified pathogens as the cause of illness. It is a map rooted in Latin words, built on pathology and mechanisms, and it is powerful. It saves lives. But it is still a map — a set of metaphors for how the body works.

As medical technology advanced, doctors became specialists. The general physician gave way to the cardiologist, the gastroenterologist, the dermatologist — each responsible for a different territory of the body. Health became a patchwork of different experts guarding their respective parts. The distance between the person who is sick and the people who hold the knowledge to help them grew wider. Specialized medicine is powerful, but it is also reductionist.

Because modern medicine focuses on parts, it can lose sight of the whole person. Side effects of drugs, especially long-term ones, are passively accepted. As the tools to suppress symptoms become more sophisticated, we can lose interest in asking why the symptoms appeared in the first place. We forget how emotions and lifestyle shape our health.

## The TCM map

Chinese medicine begins from a different premise. The body is not an isolated machine but part of a larger web of relationships — internal, emotional, seasonal, and environmental. Human bodies are interwoven with the forces of the natural world. There is no separation between subject and object. Health is "the web that has no weaver." The life force that moves through and connects the system is called [Qi](/glossary#qi).

Health, in this view, is a dynamic balance between forces — like night and day, earth and sky. It is not a fixed state to achieve but a continuous process to cultivate.

Parents pack their children's lunch boxes with sugar and chips, unknowingly shaping their gut microbiomes. College students pull all-nighters, drink until late, call instant noodles the college diet, live according to cycles of stress. Adults forget how good movement feels because they are too busy running from work to picking up their kids to cooking dinner. They forget their body is their temple until infertility, diabetes, high blood pressure, and illness slows them down. In a world where health is perceived as the absence of disease, no one blinks an eye until it is too late. Western medicine is necessary — but our lives could be improved with the wisdom of Chinese medicine alongside it.

In TCM, health is the gardener tending a garden. You sow seeds in winter, pull weeds before they overtake your seedlings, lay fertilizer when the plant is young, and water consistently. You do not wait until the garden is dying to start. You tend to it before the plants die.

## Holding both maps

Most people say, "Chinese medicine is just placebo — there's no empirical evidence." I understand the instinct. I study biology; I know what evidence means.

But you have been sitting with one map, reliant on your navigator to take you where you need to go. What if I handed you a second map — an older one, dating back three thousand years — and showed you how to read it yourself? Not to replace the first map, but to see terrain it does not show.

You might notice, holding both maps at once, that a symptom Western medicine treats as isolated — fatigue, bloating, a cold that keeps coming back — is something TCM would ask about differently: not just *what is broken*, but *what pattern is this part of*. It is worth asking both questions, not just one.

## The takeaway

The map you carry determines what you can see. If you only have one, there will always be territory you cannot find. This blog is a second set of binoculars — not a replacement for the first pair, a second one. How you define the problem determines how you solve it.
