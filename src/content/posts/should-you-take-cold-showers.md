A few of my friends begin every morning with a cold shower and a short meditation, and they swear the cold gives them a rush of dopamine. Cold water has a much longer history than the wellness trend. When I studied in Tokyo, the acupuncturist I shadowed told me about his hobby of standing under an icy waterfall, a practice called *misogi* that samurai used to purify the body and spirit. On social media, the promises have grown along with the popularity: cold showers are said to speed up your metabolism, calm inflammation, lift your mood, strengthen your immune system, and teach you discipline are some of the claims I've heard.

I went looking for the scientific evidence behind these claims and analyzed them critically from an empirical perspective and the wisdom of Traditional Chinese Medicine to offer you a more holistic view of this growing wellness trend. Here's the truth of what I found: the body reacts to cold water in measurable ways, but the proof that those reactions turn into lasting health benefits is more complicated than the internet suggests.

Cold water immersion has a spectrum of methods. At one end sits the cold shower, a couple of minutes of cold water on the skin. Further along are ice baths and plunges, which researchers call cold-water immersion, then winter swimming, which adds exercise, fresh air and often a group of friends, and finally the Wim Hof Method, which pairs cold with a breathing technique and meditation. In the largest recent review of cold-water immersion, which pooled eleven trials and 3,177 people, ten trials used baths and only one used showers (Cain et al., 2025, pp. 1, 6). Most of the science studies do not study cold showers. so whenever a headline says that cold does something, you should always ask what kind of cold exposure, for how long, and in whom was it studied in.

## What happens in your body in the first thirty seconds

When the cold water hits your skin, your brain switches on the sympathetic nervous system, the fight-or-flight branch that runs the things you do not consciously steer, like your heartbeat and breathing. Your heart rate and breathing climb, and your body releases norepinephrine, a messenger that sharpens attention, and cortisol, a hormone that mobilizes energy under stress (Cain et al., 2025, p. 2). This jolt gives the surge of dopamine and energy many crave from cold showers.

## Does Cold Water Immersion Cause Weight Loss?

In a classic experiment, a group of young men sat in water up to their necks for an hour at 14°C, and their norepinephrine rose by 530%, their dopamine (the messenger tied to motivation and reward) by 250%, and their metabolic rate (the energy the body burns just to keep going) by 350%. At a milder 20°C the metabolic rate almost doubled, and at a neutral 32°C it did not change (Šrámek et al., 2000, as cited in Mooventhan & Nivethitha, 2014, pp. 199–200). Remember that an hour at 14°C is a lot more than a two-minute shower, and the volunteers were all men, which does not explain how women who have hormonal and physiological differences may respond.

That jump in metabolic rate raises the question of whether cold helps you lose weight. Part of the answer may be brown fat, a tissue that burns energy to make heat, the way a small furnace keeps a house warm, while white fat only stores it. A 2009 study used scans that light up working tissue to examine 24 healthy men, 10 lean and 14 overweight or obese, first in a 22°C room and again during mild cold exposure at 16°C. At 22°C no brown fat was active, and in the cold it switched on in 23 of the 24 men (van Marken Lichtenbelt et al., 2009). The heavier men had less active brown fat, and those who had more active brown fat also had a higher resting metabolic rate. However, this is just a correlational observation. and no study observes the effect of cold shower on weight loss just yet.

## Can cold lift your mood?

Many social media claims say that the cold releases dopamine and endorphins, making you feel good. Winter swimmers say yes. They report feeling more vigorous, less tense and less tired (Mooventhan & Nivethitha, 2014, p. 200), but winter swimming also comes with exercise, fresh air and company, which biases results.

Controlled trials, in which researchers compare people who get the cold with people who do not, give a more complicated answer. When Cain et al. (2025) combined trials in which young men sat in 14°C baths, they found no clear change in stress right after the bath or one, 24 or 48 hours later, and a drop at twelve hours (pp. 8–9). That one result is weak. It rests on three small trials from one research group in Lithuania, with 26, 12 and 17 men, and it compares men with their own starting levels and not with a no-cold comparison group, and cortisol naturally fluctuates over the day (Cain et al., 2025, pp. 1, 7). The one trial that measured mood directly, in 20 men, found no difference between the cold group and the comparison group (Skein, 2018, as described in Cain et al., 2025, p. 11).

A larger 2026 analysis pooled 13 studies and 4,105 people and found that the results generally leaned in favor of cold water for depressive symptoms, stress and wellbeing. The estimates were imprecise, shrank when only studies with a comparison group were counted, and came mostly from studies at high risk of bias, so the authors conclude that the evidence does not establish that cold water improves mood (Ngandeu Schepanski et al., 2026). Part of the difficulty is built in, because nobody can be blinded to ice water, and expectations color how people say they feel (Cain et al., 2025, p. 11).

## Does cold sharpen your mind?

Norepinephrine is supposed to sharpen attention, so it is fair to expect cold to make you think better, but the studies the 2025 review gathers "have produced varying results" (Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 392). A systematic review of 18 experimental settings, eight using cold air and ten using cold-water immersion, found that cold made healthy people perform worse on thinking tasks in 15 of them (p. 392). A smaller study of 12 healthy people who repeated cold immersions found that their cognitive performance improved afterward (p. 392), althought the review doesn{t specify how it was repeated. One way to reconcile these results, which is my guess and not the review's, is that a first dunk sets off an alarm the brain has to deal with before it can focus, while a body with practice treats the same cold as familiar.

## Does cold keep you from getting sick?

Social media also claims that cold water immersion improves your immune system. In a 2024 randomized trial, in which volunteers are assigned to groups by chance, 60 healthy adults in Egypt took either a cold or a hot shower every day for 90 days while researchers tracked their blood (El-Ansary et al., 2024). By day 90 the cold group had more immunoglobulins, the antibodies that latch onto specific germs, and more of two cytokines, the chemical messages immune cells send each other: interleukin-2 (IL-2), which tells T cells, the immune cells that kill infected cells, to multiply, and interleukin-4 (IL-4), which helps B cells make antibodies. In the hot shower group, IgM, the first antibody the body makes against a new germ, actually fell. The trial counted molecules in the blood and not colds, so higher levels may not mean fewer infections, the comparison group took hot showers and not none, and it is a single study of 60 people.

The largest shower trial gets closer to real life. In it, 3,018 adults took cold showers of 30, 60 or 90 seconds every day for 30 days and had 29% less sickness absence from work than people who showered as usual, although they did not report fewer days of actually being ill (Buijze et al., 2016, as cited in Cain et al., 2025, p. 10). The numbers were self-reported, so cold showers may have prevented some infections, and people who start the day with a cold shower may also simply feel well enough to show up. The same trial found slightly better quality-of-life scores at 30 days that were gone by 90 days, which the authors attribute to people dropping the routine over time and to the self-reported scores (p. 11).

Swimmers tell a more mixed story. In the first study to ask ice swimmers about infections, 85 regular swimmers filled out a survey, and 40% said they caught fewer, milder and shorter upper respiratory infections after they started (Brenke, 1990, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 393). A later study that compared cold-water swimmers with their own partners and with pool swimmers found that the swimmers reported about the same number of respiratory infections as everyone else (Collier et al., 2021, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 393). Another followed open-water swimmers for six months and found that the antibodies in their blood and saliva fell while their resistance to respiratory illness did not change (Kormanovski et al., 2010, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 393). Fewer infections were also reported in 20 people with chronic lung disease after repeated cold water treatments (Goedsche et al., 2007, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 393). Most of these are surveys and small groups, and swimmers who feel sick may simply stop swimming.

Lab experiments are no clearer. The review cites a single six-week experiment, in which young men took cold immersions three times a week, both for a slight activation of the immune system, with higher IL-6 and more T cells, and for no significant change in antibodies or white blood cell counts (Jansky et al., 1996, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, pp. 392–393). In a three-week experiment with 12 men, white blood cell counts did not differ from the comparison group (Versteeg et al., 2023, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 392). The authors suggest that results differ because swimmers differ in how used to the cold they are, how cold the water is and how long they stay in, and they warn that repeated or excessive exposure without enough recovery could stress the body in a way that weakens immune function (p. 393).

A single cold dip does little on its own. Studies that measured immune cells and saliva antibodies right after immersion found no significant change, and the evidence was graded inconclusive (Cain et al., 2025, p. 8). Inflammation markers actually rose right after immersion and an hour later in two small studies, a rise the authors suggest may reflect the body releasing stored sugar for fuel more than real inflammation (pp. 8, 12). If cold helps the immune system, it looks like something that builds with repetition and not something you get on the spot.

## What about the Wim Hof Method?

The most dramatic immune result comes from the Wim Hof Method, which pairs cold with a breathing technique (rounds of deep, rapid breaths followed by breath holds) and meditation. In a 2014 Dutch experiment, 24 healthy young men were injected with endotoxin, a fragment of E. coli bacteria that makes the immune system react as if it were fighting an infection, causing fever, headache and flu-like aches without a real germ. Half of the men had trained for ten days with Wim Hof's instructors, which included standing barefoot in snow and swimming in ice-cold water, and they practiced the breathing during the experiment. Their immune response looked remarkably calm. Their levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-8, the inflammatory alarm signals that cause fever and aches, were 53%, 57% and 51% lower than in the untrained men, their level of IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine that acts like a brake, was nearly three times higher, and their peak flu-like symptoms were 56% lower (Kox et al., 2014, p. 7380). The more epinephrine (adrenaline) a man released during the breathing, the more IL-10 he made (p. 7381).

Before crediting the cold, look at what the men were doing during the test, which was breathing. The technique lowered the carbon dioxide in their blood, made it more alkaline, and dropped their oxygen saturation to around 50% for about ten seconds in each cycle (p. 7380), and the authors conclude that the breathing, more than the cold, most likely triggered the adrenaline surge (p. 7382). Because the training combined both techniques, we cannot say which part mattered. The study also involved only young men and measured a one-time response to a laboratory immune challenge and not real-life illness, and a cold shower is only one fraction of the Wim Hof Method.

## What about sore muscles?

Cold baths are especiallypopular among athletes post workouts to reduce soreness. The 2025 review calls the results small and inconsistent: some studies are positive, some inconclusive, and some find more soreness (Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 394). A pooled analysis of 44 studies found that cold-water immersion beat doing nothing at relieving soreness right after exercise, whatever the temperature, and soreness that arrives a day or two later in nearly every subgroup except longer dips (Batista et al., 2023, as cited in Kunutsor et al., 2025, p. 395). A Cochrane review of 17 small trials with 366 people also found some evidence that cold immersion reduced that delayed soreness compared with rest (Bleakley et al., 2012, as cited in p. 395).

## What the evidence adds up to

Put together, the research describes a body that reacts strongly to cold and a body of evidence that has not yet shown those reactions turn into lasting benefits. The authors of the largest review say that cold-water immersion's popularity comes with "largely unsupported claims in mainstream media about its benefits for physical health and mental wellbeing" (Cain et al., 2025, p. 11). A second, wider review that gathered studies on everything from cold showers to winter swimming ends in the same place, concluding that most of the evidence comes from small studies that do not settle the question and calling for larger, better-controlled trials (Kunutsor et al., 2025, pp. 400, 402). The best signs of benefit arrive on a delay, such as lower stress the evening after, more antibodies after three months, and less sickness absence after a month of daily showers, and most results are blood markers or self-reported scores and not diagnoses. Exposures ranged from 30 seconds to two hours in water between 7°C and 15°C (p. 6), and most volunteers were young, healthy men. Only one of the eleven trials in the main review included women (p. 13).

Sex may matter as well. In one of the few studies to compare the sexes directly, 22 men and 19 women, mostly in their twenties, did reaction-time and memory tasks on three separate days: in a 23°C room, in a 10°C room, and sitting in 15°C water up to the neck while wearing a wetsuit (Kong et al., 2024, pp. 3–4). In the cold room the women became slower on three of the tasks while the men became faster (p. 6), and only the men showed a rise in a heart-rhythm measure that tracks the stress response (pp. 6–7). The women's slowdown was clearest in the first session, and the authors suggest that women may need around 30 minutes to adjust to the cold (pp. 8, 12). They point to possible explanations, including more body fat in the women (28% compared with 20% in the men), colder hands from tighter blood vessels, and estrogen, but the study did not test any of these and did not track the menstrual cycle (pp. 12–13). The sample was small and healthy, and reaction time is not a health outcome, so the study is a reason for caution and not a verdict. If you are a woman trying cold for the first time, a practitioner might suggest starting with cool water instead of icy water, keeping it shorter than the routines you see online, and stopping when your hands go numb or the shivering will not stop.

So the Western map says that cold may be worth trying, but clinical results modestly or do not support benefits. But before you push your body through sheer discipline, the Chinese medicine map asks you to check in with your own body first.

## What Chinese medicine says

Western research asks whether cold works for the average person. Chinese medicine asks about you and your body. TCM does not care how much cold you can tolerate, but rather, whether your body is in a state to.

The framework starts with yin and yang. Cold belongs to yin, heat belongs to yang, and health is the body's ability to move fluidly between the two. A brief, controlled shock of cold pulls your energy inward, and a body with plenty of yang answers by sending warmth back out, widening the blood vessels and raising circulation. Repeated gently, that exchange trains how well your pores open and close and how freely Qi and Blood travel between the surface and the center of the body.

But in TCM too much cold that overwhelms the body becomes one of the six pathogens, the environmental forces that can invade the body's defenses. Cold slows and tightens: just as cold hands clench and cold muscles cramp, it constricts the vessels and slows the flow of Qi and Blood (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 94). Warmth drives circulation, digestion and immunity. For someone with plenty of yang fire, a minor stress of a cold shower strengthens their body. For someone with a weak yang fire, cold water is closer to a bucket thrown on a fizzling campfire that was already dying.

A weak fire has a recognizable signature. When Kidney yang, the root of your vitality, runs low, the signs include a weak lower back and knees, frequent urination, loose stools, low libido and difficulty conceiving. When Spleen yang, which digests food and turns it into energy, is weak, the picture is bloating, diarrhea and a heavy, cold, damp feeling after meals. These are the people who struggle to get up in the morning, crave sunlight, and tell themselves that it is supposed to be hard and they just need to tough it out. In TCM theory, cold that the body cannot push back can also make Blood and Moisture congeal into clots or lumps, and stagnation is where illness takes root (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991).

## One temperature, two bodies

The two maps agree that a cold shower costs the body something, and they differ in the question that follows. The Western papers describe a sympathetic stress response in which the metabolic rate climbs by 93% at 20°C and by 350% at 14°C as the body burns more fuel to hold its core temperature (Mooventhan & Nivethitha, 2014, p. 200). This could be seen as good for weight loss. TCM asks whether you have the reserves to spend, and none of the trials looked at people who run cold, tired or feel bloated and cramped. The research mostly asks whether the average healthy person benefits, and its participants were mostly young men.

Western medicine and its studies present claims towards the generalized, average person. It is universal and convenient. Chinese Medicine, while much more tedious, looks at each person as a unique system. There is no doubt that cold showers increase alertness, which may convince a tired body that the shower is good for it. It is like fanning a fire: you will certainly stimulate the flame, but the question is whether your fire can sustain it or be wiped out.

If you run warm, digest well, have strong circulation and show no chronic signs of cold, cold exposure may genuinely benefit you. A practitioner might call that robust yang, and you might call it good discipline. If you are always cold, tired after meals, bloated, prone to menstrual cramps and clots, and slow to warm up in the morning, a cold shower may not be building anything and may be depleting what you have. A practitioner might suggest warming instead, with ginger tea, a foot soak before bed, moxa or cooked, warming foods. Not because you are weak-minded, but because your fire needs tending, not testing. Temperature is a tool, not a test of character, and what strengthens one body may quietly drain another.

Also, TCM strongly pushes for warmth in days leading up to and throughout the menstrual cycle to promote blood flow and decrease menstrual cramps. This includes prioritizing warm baths or showers and hot foot soaks and not cold showers.

Next time you see a social media post about a morning cold shower or a cold plunge after the gym, ask yourself what your body actually needs right now: to be challenged, or to be warmed?
