My boyfriend is Latino, which means coffee is not a beverage in our house — it's a daily ritual. Every morning he grinds the beans, heats the water to exactly the right temperature, presses the French press with great care and patience. The smell fills the apartment with the glory of mornings. I'll be honest: he's converted me from anti-coffee to loving it. That first sip — dark, rich, a little bitter — feels like the day has officially started.

My dad's version is less romantic. He wakes up, tears open an instant coffee packet, dumps in sugar, and chugs it standing in the kitchen. No ritual. Just fuel.

Both of them drink it the same way: before eating anything.

They're not alone. Coffee is the most widely consumed psychoactive substance on the planet, and for most people it's the first thing that touches their stomach each morning. For those who intermittently fast, it's commonly pushed for— black coffee has about two calories per cup, doesn't trigger a meaningful insulin response, doesn't interrupt autophagy, and suppresses appetite long enough to extend the fasting window (Clinical Nutrition ESPEN, 2021). From a pure fasting standpoint, coffee is amazing.

## First: Coffee Is Not the Villain

The research on coffee's benefits is substantial. A meta-analysis in *Scientific Reports* found that regular coffee drinkers had a 35% lower risk of significant liver fibrosis, with five or more cups per day associated with 47% lower liver cancer risk and 42% lower liver-related mortality (Sang et al., 2016). A study tracking 48,000 people over four years found that increasing coffee by just one cup per day lowered type 2 diabetes risk by 11% (Bhupathiraju et al., 2014). A separate meta-analysis of 30 epidemiological studies found each daily cup reduces diabetes risk by roughly 6% (Ding et al., 2014). Observational studies consistently link moderate intake to lower risk of Parkinson's and Alzheimer's — a 2018 dose-response meta-analysis in *Nutrients* found a significant inverse association for Alzheimer's, though a Mendelian randomization study complicates the causal picture (Liu et al., 2016). And coffee happens to be the largest source of antioxidants in the average Western diet — ahead of fruits and vegetables.

But it helps to understand what coffee is actually doing when it makes you feel awake. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain. Adenosine is a neurotransmitter that accumulates the longer you are awake — it is the body's own sleep pressure signal, building quietly in the background until you feel tired enough to rest. Caffeine does not remove the adenosine. It sits in the receptor so that adenosine cannot bind and prevents your brain from receiving the signal that you are tired. So the alertness you feel after a cup of coffee is not new energy. It is your brain temporarily unable to register that it is tired. The adenosine keeps building behind the blockade, and when the caffeine wears off — usually after five to six hours — all of that accumulated adenosine hits the receptors at once. That is the crash. Coffee does not give you energy. It borrows from your future reserves, and Chinese Medicine asks what western medicine does not ask: whether your body is prepared or not.

So the issue is not coffee itself. It's when, how much, and on what kind of stomach. And this is where both Western research and Chinese medicine have something to say.

## What Happens When Coffee Hits an Empty Stomach

## The Western Lens

There are a few disadvantages to drinking on an empty tank, and they compound each other.

**Your blood sugar response gets worse, not better.** A 2020 study at the University of Bath, published in the *British Journal of Nutrition*, found that drinking strong black coffee before breakfast increased the blood glucose response to that meal by approximately 50% (Sheridan et al., 2020). Caffeine impairs insulin sensitivity — so when you finally do eat, your body handles the sugar significantly less efficiently. For someone fasting specifically for metabolic health, this is a problem worth considering.

**Your stomach floods with acid that has nothing to buffer it.** Coffee activates bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) in gastric parietal cells, directly triggering hydrochloric acid secretion — and not just from the caffeine. Chlorogenic acid and other roasted compounds contribute independently (Liszt et al., 2017). With food in the stomach, that acid has something to work on. Without food, it sits there. That's the heartburn. That's the nausea. That's the churning some may feel.

**You stack caffeine on top of your body's own cortisol peak.** Cortisol — the stress hormone — naturally surges 30 to 45 minutes after waking. It's how your body prepares its engine for the day. A moderate amount is important to start the day, but too much of it becomes stressful for the body. Caffeine amplifies that peak. A 2020 study in *Nutrients* found that women are significantly more sensitive to this cortisol amplification than men, which matters because chronically elevated cortisol disrupts estrogen, progesterone, and the stress-recovery cycle (Penaforte et al., 2020). Habitual drinkers build partial tolerance, but if you're already running on poor sleep and stress, coffee first thing makes the hole deeper.

**Caffeine absorbs faster with nothing in the way.** Without food slowing the process, caffeine hits the bloodstream harder and crashes steeper.

## The Chinese Medicine Lens

In TCM, the question isn't "what's in the coffee?" It's "what state is your body in and what does that coffee do to your state?"

Excess heat and dampness in the body are considered “pathogens” or factors that cause disbalance and can lead to emergent health issues. Some individuals are naturally prone to excess heat and dampness and some are not.

**Coffee produces Heat.** Beinfield and Korngold list it alongside sugar, alcohol, adrenaline, and spicy food as a substance that generates internal Heat. For someone who already runs hot — red face, irritability, dry mouth, constipation — coffee makes the condition worse (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 65).

**Coffee generates Dampness.** This one surprises people, because coffee feels like a food that promotes drying effects on the body. Coffee is grouped with alcohol, sugar, and dairy as foods that "generate Dampness and promote swelling." Try having warm, cooked foods that gently nourish to minimize the need for "the quick energy provided by stimulants and refined carbohydrates" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 199).

**Coffee cravings tell you something about your Spleen.** Chapter 15 of *Between Heaven and Earth* puts it plainly: "If the Spleen is undermined by Cold and deficiency, cravings for foods like coffee, chocolate, and alcohol are likely to occur. These are warming, bitter foods that stimulate the Liver and Heart, producing a temporary feeling of increased energy and circulation" (p. 330). The borrowed energy creates more heat. If the coffee comes with sugar and milk, it also creates phlegm, which causes stagnation in the body.

**Coffee depletes Yin.** This is the long game. Coffee is warm-to-hot in thermal nature and bitter in flavor — which in five-element terms means it enters the Heart channel, mobilizing Qi and creating a feeling of alertness and drive. But it does this by *spending* rather than *building*. Over time, Heat consumes Moisture, dries the Blood, and destabilizes the Shen — the spirit that the Heart houses. In the case study of Hector, a Fire-type personality, the advice is to "avoid overindulging in heating and drying foods such as curry, sugar, alcohol, coffee, tea, chili, and salt" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 182). Coffee is like pressing the gas button and consuming a lot of your fuel. Instead, you should build energy by adding fuel to the tank through eating a warm and easy-to-digest breakfast like the Taiwanese breakfast I loved when studying in Taipei: egg omelet folded with various meats inside with warm, freshly made soy milk.

**And in the morning, the timing is the worst part.** The Spleen and Stomach are quiet when you wake up — not yet warm, not yet active. Their digestive fire is still building. Sending a hot, bitter, acidic liquid into that empty space with no food to anchor it is like throwing kindling onto coals that haven't caught yet. It flares, it smokes, and the fire doesn't hold. The Stomach needs warm, nourishing food first — congee, oats, soup, eggs — to build its fire properly for the day.

## How to Make Coffee Work for You

I’m not telling you to stop drinking coffee. I’m reminding you that the order matters and to take a look at your body constitution (maybe with the help of a TCM practitioner) to determine if you should be drinking less.

Eat first. Even something small — a warm bowl of oatmeal, eggs, a handful of nuts, a slice of toast. Give the Stomach something to work with before you demand it to work hard.

Wait 60 to 90 minutes after waking before your first cup, so cortisol has time to descend from its natural peak. Coffee after that window enhances alertness rather than stacking on top of a stress response your body was already managing.

Notice what coffee actually does to *you*. Does it make you jittery or focused? Does it help your digestion move or lock it up? Do you crash at 2pm? Do you sleep well? The answers are different for different constitutions — someone who runs cold and sluggish will experience coffee differently than someone who runs hot and anxious. TCM doesn't give one-size-fits-all prescriptions, and the same goes for coffee and all foods.

## The Takeaway

I still drink coffee. I just don't drink it first anymore. My boyfriend still does his French press ceremony — and I’m still nudging him to have a toast before the first sip \*wink\*. My dad is a hard sell.

The point isn't to demonize a drink that billions of people love and that the research shows has real benefits. The point is that a cup of coffee at 10am after breakfast and a cup of coffee at 6am on an empty stomach are not the same thing — not to your blood sugar, not to your cortisol, not to your stomach, not to your Qi. The ritual can stay.
