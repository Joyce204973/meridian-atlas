## What’s the Craze with Low-Carb Diet?

*Low-carb* diets are increasingly popular for weight-loss. “Keto diet” was the most googled diet in 2020 (McGaugh & Barthel, 2022). While it’s true that low-carb diets are indeed effective for weight loss, social media can be a dangerous place for young people to mimic the diets of others with completely different bodies and lifestyles. Diets, whether low-carb, keto, calorie deficit, or intermittent fasting are indeed effective for weight loss and other health benefits, but the craze with weight loss has villainized carbs for many uninformed individuals in pursuit of a social media body image. This blog speaks to the girls and guys out there who have developed a fear of carbs and eat from a place of restriction and control without understanding.

First, studies show that it’s true dieting can cause weight loss, although there isn’t one way that is better than another. A review of 61 randomized trials looking at 6,925 adults found that participants following a low-carb diet lost only about 1kg more weight than those eating a diet with a normal amount of carbs over three and eight months (Naudé et al., 2022, Abstract). In another trial of 609 adults over 12 months, those eating a low-carb diet lost 6 kg and those eating a low-fat diet lost 5.3 kg, which is not a significant difference. (Gardner et al., 2018, Abstract). In both studies, the difference in following a low carb diet versus another diet is very small, but both only look at results under a year. Other limitations are that the papers do not differentiate between individuals who are overweight or diabetic from those who are not or consider the differences between men and women.

So why do different diets work for weight loss? Most likely because they ate fewer calories than they burn, which is called a calorie deficit. In 45 of the 61 trials, both groups were given a similar calorie target, whether they were cutting carbs or not (Naudé et al., 2022, Abstract).

## Carbs Fuel Your Brain and Muscles

Carbohydrates are made of lego blocks of glucose.

Your brain normally runs on glucose as its primary fuel (Tufts University, 2008). When carbs are scarce for long enough, such as during longer fasts, cells turn to making ketones made from fat to use as energy. Ketones can supply up to 60% of the brain’s energy (García-Rodríguez & Giménez-Cassina, 2021, Introduction, p. 1). However, low carb availability in the blood stream may impair cognition. In a small study, 19 women chose either a near-zero-carb diet or a reduced-calorie balanced diet. During the first days without carbs, the low-carb group did worse on memory tasks, and their performance improved once carbs came back (D’Anci et al., 2009). However, the low-carb group also responded faster on an attention task and reported feeling less confused (D’Anci et al., 2009). A limitation of the study was that the sample was tiny.

Muscles also use carbohydrates as their main fuel. They store their reserve of glucose in an on-site source called glycogen (Lodge et al., 2023, p. 4). The harder your muscles work during exercise, the more your muscles lean on them (Lodge et al., 2023, section 5.2, p. 10). For those in a ketogenic state from following the ketogenic diet or prolonged fasting, there is little carbohydrate available, so the body shifts toward burning fat (Lodge et al., 2023, p. 5). Athletes in nutritional ketosis show more fat burning in their muscles (García-Rodríguez & Giménez-Cassina, 2021, p. 3). However, carbs make energy faster than fat does (Lodge et al., 2023, p. 5), and when glycogen runs low, athletes get tired sooner and their intensity drops (Lodge et al., 2023, section 4.2.2, p. 9), and fat produces energy more slowly (Lodge et al., 2023, p. 5).

A 2023 review looked at 12 studies on endurance athletes. It linked low carbohydrate availability to more injuries, especially bone stress injuries (Lodge et al., 2023).  But of the 12 studies, several included only men, and none looked at women only (Lodge et al., 2023, Abstract, p. 1).

Before you reach for your chips and donuts, not all carbs fuel your body the same. Complex carbs and starches like rice, sweet potatoes, whole-wheat bread fuel your body for a longer, sustained period while refined carbs like pastries and other processed snacks provide a quick rush of energy followed by a crash (Pitchford, 2002, p. 457).

## Why do Women Need Carbs?

Women should not blindly follow the low-carb diets that their male friends and partners follow because the majority of studies on diets neglect to consider the biological and hormonal differences between women and men. Women, to support their power to create life, have hormones that fluctuate across the monthly menstrual cycle.

Maybe you have noticed yourself or heard about other women who suddenly want to eat more food and especially more carbs in the week leading up to their period. This isn’t because you lack discipline. There is a hormonal and biological reason behind what your body is telling you.

Estrogen is a hormone that tells the uterus lining to grow and thicken. It rises during ovulation, around day 14, and then again during the luteal phase, which is around day 15 to 28 of a 28-day menstrual cycle. Higher estrogen levels turn down a back up system the body uses to make glucose called gluconeogenesis. Gluconeogenesis is the liver’s process of making new glucose from non-carb precursors: lactate, glycerol, and amino acids like alanine. It’s the backup system to have glucose available in the blood. When you run low on dietary carbs or liver glycogen, or you’re deep into a long workout, gluconeogenesis keeps blood glucose from dropping so your brain and muscles still have fuel. So when estrogen is higher in the luteal phase, the body makes less of its own glucose and leans more on glucose it already has or eats. During longer or harder exercise, especially if intake is low (LCA in your passage), blood glucose is more likely to dip and performance may suffer.(Lodge et al., 2023, section 5.2, p. 10). What’s more is that the luteinizing hormone (LH) whose job is to drive ovulation and regulate hormone production across the different stages may depend on carbohydrate availability. There may be lower athletic performance in the luteal phase, which is the week leading up to the period, if there is less carbohydrates available. Studies also show that eating more carbohydrates in the mid luteal phase (roughly 4 to 9 days after ovulation which is days 18-23 of the cycle) increases glycogen stores in the muscle by about 13% which may help with sports performance. This means that a high-carbohydrate snack three to four hours before exercise may help offset the lower gluconeogenesis rates in the luteal phase (Lodge et al., 2023, section 5.2, p. 10). Women are not small men and must take care that they have sufficient carbs for hormone regulation.

## How Losing Weight Differs in Women and Men

Cutting calories does not work the same in men and women. In an 8-week low-calorie diet of about 810 kcal a day in people with pre-diabetes, both sexes lost weight, but men lost more: 11.8% of body weight versus 10.3% in women (Christensen et al., 2018).

Many women cut carbs for weight loss, but weight loss is not a pure calories-in and calories–out scale. Many feel like they do everything right: sleep, nutrition, and exercise, but still the numbers won’t drop on the scale. That’s because there’s an important piece missing: regulated hormones. When you eat glucose, the cells need to respond to a signal called insulin to take the glucose from the blood into the cells. When women follow a calorie-deficit or cut carbs but are constantly stressed in their daily lives and add on top the stress of a toxic relationship with food, cortisol increases. Cortisol is the hormone your adrenal glands release under stress. When cortisol stays high for a long time, cells may respond less to insulin, the hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells. As a result, fat may collect around the abdomen (Paredes & Ribeiro, 2014). Fasting is another stressor. In a 48-hour fast with 14 men and 14 women, norepinephrine (another stress hormone) and perceived stress rose only in women (Solianik et al., 2024). Over longer periods, an observational study of 3,617 US adults found that women with more childhood stress gained BMI faster across 15 years, and men’s BMI did not follow stress the same way (Liu & Umberson, 2015). While these studies don’t show that restrictive eating causes women to hold on to fat, they show that women and men may respond differently to calorie deficits and dieting. So for the ladies out there who are trying to drop a few kilograms, prioritize hormonal regulation as well, not just calorie restriction. Regulation and restriction are two different verbs, and the female body does not lose weight as well when it does not feel safe.

## Western Map Vs TCM Map

In the west, health-conscious “granola” moms look at clean eating as fresh, unrefined, no artificial ingredients, low in cholesterol, calories, sugar, and salt. Raw diet saves you from eating “dead” and “toxic” meats, low-carb and keto diets help you burn fat, gym-bros reach for macro goals in proteins, and other diets exist for cardiovascular diseases. Each one is controversial with each other: raw eating prioritizes raw fruits and vegetables, which contain a lot of carbs that the low-carb and keto folks minimize. Keto folks eat a lot of healthy fats that those on diets for cardiovascular diseases avoid. Gym bros trying to reach calorie goals eat a lot of cooked complex carbs and proteins that raw diets push away. The one thing that all these diets have in common? They are all one-size-fit-all.

In the TCM map, everything is relational and interactive. Food comes from nature and thus has characteristics that change depending on context, such as how it is cooked. The appropriateness of a food for a person looks at whether the person has a strong or weak constitution, congested or depleted body, Hot or Cold body, and the climate (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991). Rather than following the restrictive eating of western diets, TCM advocates for a more intuitive approach, following the needs of the body.

## What Traditional Chinese Medicine Says About Carbs

We are all familiar with how modern society categorizes food: carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, and minerals. 1 gram of carb is 4 calories, 1 gram of protein is 4 calories, and a gram of fat is 9 calories. In Chinese medicine, food is not looked at with their calories in mind, but rather their function, because *food is medicine*. Carbohydrates are not perceived as making you fat, but rather they serve important functions – when eaten in moderation and correctly of course (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 15, Food Ideology West and East). The food you consume is transported and transformed by the spleen, which is like the digestive fire, and extracts gu-qi (food-qi) to fuel your qi and blood (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 443), which gives you life and energy. Complex carbs include grains, like rice, barley, millet and sweet starches like yams and sweet potatoes. These carbs have the important job of nourishing the body (Kohn, 2010, chapter “Medicinal Diets,” PDF p. 16). Their nature is also sweet, so it strengthens the spleen and stomach systems (Kohn, 2010, p. 26 and increase Qi and Moisture, which are good for the body especially during the drier winter season (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 15, Congestion). Especially for those with deficiency and weakness patterns in the body, naturally sweet carbs tonify, or strengthen, the body (Pitchford, 2002, p. 188).

My Chinese grandparents were farmers, and they understand the importance of food. Whenever I fall sick, my grandma cooks me a warm bowl of congee. It is packed with the gentle nourishment of pork, but the important part?  – It’s the rice. Gently broken down during simmering, the soft grain easily gets digested to nourish the body so that my cells can fight the virus. During winter months, a warm bowl of congee, made with either rice or millet, is a popular breakfast to fuel the body with warmth and nutrients first thing in the morning. The millet strengthens the spleen, which is the digestive fire of the body.

Different carbohydrates do different jobs. Kohn says rice is the most neutral, wheat tends to cool, and millet warms (Kohn, 2010, p. 26). In TCM, cravings for sweet carbs is a sign of liver deficiency, and one should turn to sweet vegetables such as sweet potatoes for long-lasting sustained energy rather than the quick-high of refined carbohydrates. This also helps to ease sugar cravings (Pitchford, 2002, p. 190). However, you must be careful to not eat too much sweet foods, because excess warm, fatty, sweet foods causes damp-heat in the spleen, which essentially knocks the body out of balance.

## Where the two maps agree

Both maps separate refined carbohydrate from whole carbohydrate. Refined carbohydrates such as white flour and sugar are the usual culprits in weight gain and recommends replacing them with fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes (Zelman, 2004, So Simple, Yet So Complex). Beinfield and Korngold contrast “convenient carbohydrates like bread and pasta” with complex ones like rice and legumes (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 8), and Pitchford makes the same distinction between whole-food and refined sugars (Pitchford, 2002, p. 189). Both also warn against excess fat alongside sweet food: WebMD notes that refined carbohydrate foods often carry added fats and extra calories (Zelman, 2004, So Simple, Yet So Complex), and Chen names fatty, sweet foods as a cause of damp-heat (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 458).

Both also argue that it is not a one-diet-fits-all. The Lodge review concludes that carbohydrate recommendations were built on research that largely leaves out women and fails to consider the menstrual cycle (Lodge et al., 2023, section 8, p. 11). Beinfield and Korngold say a complex-carbohydrate, low-fat diet suits an overweight diabetic with excess Heat and Dampness, while a weak, emaciated, Dry diabetic may need protein and high-quality fats (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 15, Food Ideology West and East).

What Chinese medicine adds is the function in the body: carbohydrate as raw material for the Spleen’s transformation into qi, and notices who is eating it (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 443; Kohn, 2010, p. 26; Pitchford, 2002, p. 457). Western medicine, as these sources present it, describes carbohydrate as fuel for muscle and brain and as a share of calories: the Institute of Medicine range of 45% to 65% of calories (Zelman, 2004, So Simple, Yet So Complex) and 55–75% for athletes (Lodge et al., 2023, section 3.3, p. 5).
