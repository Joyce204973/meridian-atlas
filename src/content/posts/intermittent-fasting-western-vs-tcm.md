What if I told you that you could eat just as much as you normally eat, but if you restrict those calories to a smaller window, you could observe benefits such as weight loss, enhanced brain function, decreased glucose levels, enhanced cellular repair, increased energy, improved learning and memory? You could literally have more energy, lose fat, and get smarter, and feel better.

## What is intermittent fasting?

Intermittent fasting (IF) is a style of eating that squeezes your food into a smaller window of time. The most common version is to eat for 8 hours and fast for the other 16. Some people shrink the window to 6 or even 4 hours. Others fast for 24 hours a few days a week. On the 5:2 plan, people eat about 500 to 700 calories on two days of the week and eat normally on the other five. On alternate-day fasting (ADF), people eat normally one day and fast the next (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2542).

## Why Fast?

In today’s society, the abundance of food results in a cultural norm of. three meals a day and snacks, and overeating as a result of stress. This results in a digestive system that is always breaking down food, leaving the body no time for rest and repair.

Intermittent fasting mimics the eating habits of our ancestors, in which food was not always available (Cabo and Mattson, 2019, p. 2543). The concept of break-fast, as in breaking a fasted state upon waking in the morning, was not always around. Our ancestors would have to wake up and go hunt for food before filling their bellies. When the body is not breaking down food, it has the energy to start repairing itself.

Now a note from my experience: Fasting isn’t just skipping meals. It is about being intentional with what you consume: food, social media, thoughts. When you can’t reach for your snacks to cope with stress or fatigue, you start tuning into your body, and start being intentional with your habits and thoughts. When your body no longer needs to process  food around the clock, you become more in tune to your body, more intuitive of your body’s needs, and more respectful of it. Fasting teaches you discipline and intentionality to create a life you want.

## What happens inside you: the metabolic switch

After a meal, your cells burn glucose (sugar) for fuel. Once you've gone roughly 8 to 14 hours without food, your liver runs low on its stored sugar and your body starts burning fat instead. The liver turns fatty acids into ketone bodies, small fuel molecules of fuel (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, pp. 2542–2543). In this metabolic switch, ketone levels start rising 8 to 12 hours into a fast and reach 2 to 5 mM by 24 hours (p. 2542). Ketones are more than fuel. They also act as messengers that change how many other proteins in the cell behave, including ones linked to health and aging (p. 2543).

## The benefits

### Weight and body fat

Fasting lowers insulin, the hormone that tells your cells to pull glucose out of the blood and store it. In one small trial, 16 healthy people did alternate-day fasting for 22 days. They lost 2.5% of their body weight and 4% of their fat mass, and their fasting insulin fell 57% (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2545). When trials compare fasting against ordinary daily calorie cutting, the two show similar results in weight loss alone. A meta-analysis of 11 trials (850 people, 67% women) found about 7% weight loss with intermittent restriction versus 5% with daily restriction in six months (Harvie & Haiba, 2026, p. 3). Thus, the strengths in IF lie in the other benefits of a ketogenic state, not just weight loss.

### Blood sugar and insulin

IF can reduce blood sugar and insulin levels. In two trials of about 100 women who were overweight each, one group followed the 5:2 plan and the other cut daily calories by 25%. After six months both groups had lost the same amount of weight, but the 5:2 group had a bigger gain in insulin sensitivity and a bigger drop in waist size (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2545). Daily calorie cutting or 4:3 fasting (24-hour fasts three times a week) also reversed insulin resistance in people with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes (p. 2546).

### Heart health

Intermittent fasting improved blood pressure, resting heart rate, cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose and insulin, plus markers of inflammation and oxidative stress, in animals and in humans (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2546).

### Cellular repair

When glucose, amino acids and insulin are all low, a protein complex called mTOR turns down. mTOR is the cell's main growth switch. When it's on, the cell builds protein. When it's off, the cell pauses building and starts recycling (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2544). That recycling is called autophagy, which means "self-eating." Cells begin autophagy 24-48 hours into a fasted state. The cell breaks down worn-out proteins and damaged mitochondria (its power plants) and recycles the parts. A second sensor, AMPK, notices low energy and also triggers repair. Fasting also activates sirtuins, enzymes that help the cell build new mitochondria and resist stress (Fig. 1; Fraile-Martínez et al., 2026, p. 6). It’s important to acknowledge that most results were shown in rodents.

### The brain

IF has been shown to improve brain health, memory, and learning by changing brain signals and structure. Rodents on intermittent fasting did better on spatial, associative and working memory tasks. In obese or diabetic animals, fasting reverses the damage that obesity, diabetes and brain inflammation do to learning and memory (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2546).

One mechanism is brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a protein that helps neurons survive, grow and form connections (synaptic plasticity). Those connections are how we learn and remember. Ketones switch on the BDNF gene in neurons, which is shown to help brain health in neurodegenerative disorders (Fig. 2, p. 2544). Fasting boosts GABA signaling, the brain's calming signal, which can prevent seizures and excitotoxicity, when neurons are overstimulated and damaged (p. 2547). Both findings come mainly from animal work. Fasting slowed tumors and delayed Alzheimer's- and Parkinson's-like disease in animals (p. 2547). From cutting calories, older adults improved verbal memory, adults with mild cognitive impairment improved after 12 months, and a 2-year trial improved working memory (p. 2546).

## What Chinese medicine says about meal timing

We have all heard “breakfast is the most important meal of the day,” so is intermittent fasting good for you? Fear not, Traditional Chinese Medicine has some pointers.

In TCM, the body has a 24-hour clock. Because TCM views the body as a whole system, it recognizes that the body has finite energy, and the Qi and blood flow through the body in rhythmic patterns throughout the day, where certain biological processes are stronger at certain hours. Every two hours, Qi and blood gather in a different organ channel. The Stomach channel is strongest from 7 to 9 am and the Spleen channel from 9 to 11 am (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991). Both organs run digestion, so the morning is when food is easiest to process. The late evening is for rest, not for digesting a heavy meal. This is why many Chinese people eat dinner early and walk after their meals. Their stomachs are mostly empty by bedtime, and the digestive system gets a rest before breakfast.  TCM is rooted in Daoist philosophy, and there is a Daoist practice called Guo Wu Bu Shi” (過午不食), meaning not eating after the noon. TCM describes the day in two halves. Morning and midday are the Yang phase, when Qi flows outward to the limbs, muscles and digestive organs, and the body's "digestive fire" burns strongest \[source needed\]. As the sun drops, energy turns inward toward Yin, the phase for rest and maintenance. This Daoist practice of places eating during Yang hours, and lets the body rest and repair during Yin (Tang, 2025). This correlates with research findings that autophagy and cellular repair increase during times of fasting. So, consider intermittent fasting in an earlier window of the day, when your energy needs are higher, and the body’s energy is ready to digest, compared to eating before bed.

Eating with The Circadian Rhythm

Modern science doesn't recognize Qi the way TCM describes it, but the circadian rhythm presents similar understanding. Melatonin, the hormone that marks the biological night, rises about half an hour before bedtime (Lopez-Minguez et al., 2019, p. 7). Glucose handling changes across the day (p. 7). Cortisol, the stress hormone, follows a daily pattern (p. 6). So do hunger, with the hunger hormone ghrelin higher in the biological evening than the morning (p. 9), and the temperature of the skin (p. 6). The review's authors add that the timing of meals can set the clocks of the stomach, gut, liver, pancreas and fat tissue, based mostly on animal studies (abstract, p. 1). None of this proves the TCM clock. But both point the same way: eat more earlier, eat less late.

### Eating with Energy Needs

We should eat according to when our energy needs are higher, which is usually the first half of the day, when it is light out. Mice that were fed with a high-fat diet during the “right” feeding time (when they are physically active) gained less weight than those fed with a similar high fat diet but during the “wrong” period (when they sleep) (Lopez-Minguez et al., 2019).

Eating with Your Circadian Rhythm

It is healthier to eat in alignment with the circadian rhythm. Practicing IF but eating in a later window, such as from 2pm-10pm and then sleeping soon after is more detrimental than eating in an earlier window like 10am-4pm.

Melatonin is a hormone known for its central role in the circadian system. It increases half an hour before usual bedtime as a signal to our bodies that it is time to sleep, and it may be involved in the detrimental effects of late eating on glucose metabolism. It has been shown that melatonin administration (5 mg) worsens glucose tolerance (p. 7). A study in 40 overweight/obese women of European ancestry who were habitual late eaters showed that those who ate dinner within 2.5 h of their bedtime, when melatonin levels naturally rise, had a decrease in glucose tolerance. Eating in the two hours before bed made evening types five times more likely to be obese (p. 8)

In a study, 420 adults with obesity at nutrition clinics were followed through a weight-loss program. Those who ate their main meal, lunch, after 3 pm lost less weight than those who ate before 3 pm. The two groups had similar age, appetite hormones, calorie intake, calories burned, sleep time and mix of nutrients (Garaulet et al., 2013, as described in Lopez-Minguez et al., 2019, p. 2). The late eaters were more often evening people who skipped breakfast (Table 1, p. 3). The study only observed people, so they can't prove that lunch timing caused the difference (p. 6). There is stronger evidence in a randomized crossover trial of 32 young women. Each woman ate identical meals with lunch at 1 pm in one phase and at 4:30 pm in the other. Eating late lowered glucose tolerance, calories burned at rest and carbohydrate burning, and flattened the normal daily cortisol curve (Bandín et al., 2015, as described in the same review, p. 6). This small trial only looked at eating times and did not include fasting.

A Note for Athletes and Women

Does fasting reduce lean mass? Studies show it does not if you pair it with strength training. One small trial found that young men who fasted 16 hours a day lost fat and kept their muscle during two months of resistance training (de Cabo & Mattson, 2019, p. 2546). Another randomized controlled trial in healthy females who strength trained found that IF did not impair their muscle growth and performance (Tinsley et al., 2019).

It’s important to acknowledge that women are not small men. Women should not blindly follow IF protocols male follow and to instead be mindful of their menstrual cycles, hormone levels, and stress tolerance. Most claims on IF are based on studies on men, who have 24-hour cycles of hormones. Many studies and claims do not consider the women’s 28-day hormonal cycle. A few limited studies suggest that women and men share several benefits of IF, but it appears that women have greater sensitivity of reproductive and neuroendocrine function in response to the energetic stress of fasting (Fraile-Martínez et al., 2026). For example, women are able to tolerate less stress during the high hormone stages of the luteal cycle, which is usually the week before bleeding. In one study, women who ate with a moderate calorie deficit did not see significant disruptions in their luteinizing hormone, the signal for ovulation and a menstrual bleed, but women who had a bigger deficit did (Loucks & Thuma, 2003). On the other hand, non-randomized trials suggest women with PCOS may see better hormone levels with IF (Jirapak et al., 2026).

The key message is to not blindly follow wellness trends and to suck it up through the difficult fasting periods because “it’s supposed to be hard” and “you just need to be more disciplined.” Be mindful of your fasting window as well. Especially for women and athletes, be mindful of how you feel and your energy. While IF provides a myriad of benefits, fasting may not be suited for everyone. For example, in TCM, eating at irregular times and going through periods of feasting and fasting may be detrimental to those who have spleen qi deficiency (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991).
