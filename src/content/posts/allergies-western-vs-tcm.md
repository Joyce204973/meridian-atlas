Spring comes, petals bloom, pollen flies, and the sneezing grows. Watery, itchy eyes plague the people who suffer from seasonal allergies. Others have to avoid certain foods for fear of an allergic reaction. Why are some people cursed with allergies to pollen or food while others remain immune? What can we do to decrease the stuffy nose, the itchy eyes and the food reactions? Western medicine hands you an antihistamine pill. TCM approaches with a few needles and herbs. Let's look at how they both work.

## How Traditional Chinese Medicine views allergies

When acupuncturists talk about allergies in TCM, they don't focus on the external environment like the pollen or the food that causes the symptoms. It starts from a different picture of the body: a balanced system in which Qi (pronounced "chee", the energy that moves, warms and protects everything in the body) flows through organ networks. Health means the flow stays in balance. Anything outside the body that pushes the system out of balance counts as a pathogen, whether it is wind, cold, heat, dampness or something you inhaled. Allergy is not seen as the result of a pollen or dairy but rather a symptom of the body's imbalance that cannot handle the pollen or food.

One kind of Qi matters most for allergy. Defensive Qi (wei qi) is Qi that circulates near the surface and handles "adapting to influences such as weather or mobilizing resistance to pathogenic microorganisms and noxious substances in the environment" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 3). Think of it as the border guards of a city. When the patrol is well staffed, pollen and drafts pass by but the city guards take care of it When the guards are weak, same pollen walks in and the city reacts.

The Lung is the organ network that staffs that border. In TCM the Lung governs the skin, the nose and the outer layer of the body, and is easily disturbed from outside. "Nasal blockage and discharge commonly result from Lung and defensive qi deficiency with concomitant retention of pathogens in the nose and Lung, which render a person liable to frequent nasal stuffiness and discharge, sensitivity to dust and pollen, sneezing etc." (Deadman et al., 2007, p. 266). The emphasis is on the weakness at the border. The pollen is simply the trigger, but an external pathogen is out of our control, so TCM looks at how we can strengthen the border to alleviate the symptoms of allergies. This will be discussed further later.

TCM also explains from a philosophical perspective why allergies cluster in spring and autumn. In the Five Phases (a seasonal map that pairs each organ with one of the five elements and a phase of nature), spring belongs to Wood and the Liver, autumn to Metal and the Lung. The Lung and Liver networks are yin yang pairs, meaning they are connected. Beinfield writes that "allergies or depression that occur in the spring and fall are a manifestation of a transitional conflict between the Liver and Lung within the body and the Phases of Wood and Metal in nature" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 13). In plain terms, when the season turns, the body's internal turnover has to keep step with it, and an allergy is what a missed step looks like.

## How modern medicine sees allergies

Modern immunology breaks down why and how allergic reactions occur at the molecular level. The immune system has to attack dangerous things and learn to ignore harmless ones, called tolerance. Most harmless foreign material is easy to handle. Food and the microbes that live on you are all foreign, yet all must be left alone. An allergy is a failure of that tolerance. The immune system treats a harmless protein, such as birch pollen or peanut, as if it were dangerous.

On the first exposure, a grain of pollen lands on the lining of your nose. The cells there recognize something foreign and release alarm chemicals called IL-25, IL-33 and TSLP (Carnazza et al., 2025). Dendritic cells, the immune system's police grabs the pollen, runs it to a lymph node and shows it to a T cell that has never seen it before. In an allergic person, that T cell becomes a Th2 cell and sends out two messages, IL-4 and IL-13. Those messages tell B cells to switch from making IgM, the "all-purpose" first antibody, to making IgE. The IgE then docks onto mast cells, which sit in your skin, airways and gut (Carnazza et al., 2025). This exposure does not trigger a physiological response in the body that you notice.

In the second exposure, the pollen comes back and bridges two IgE antibodies on the same mast cell. Within minutes, the cell releases its granules, and histamine increases vascular permeability, so plasma leaks into the surrounding tissue, and it causes smooth muscle contraction, called bronchoconstriction (Thangam et al., 2018).

Histamine works by docking onto receptors, called H1, on nearby cells. The vascular permeability causes redness and swelling, tightening muscle around the airways causes a tight chest and wheeze, and overactive glands creates extra mucus. Histamine increases itchiness. Almost every immediate allergic reaction can be switched on through H1 (Thangam et al., 2018). Histamine binding onto H1 pulls Th2 cells toward the allergen, and through the H4 receptor it recruits more mast cells and eosinophils (Thangam et al., 2018).

Allergies are very common. In a 2021 US survey, nearly 1 in 3 adults and more than 1 in 4 children reported a seasonal allergy, eczema or food allergy (American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology \[ACAAI\], 2024, citing CDC data). Among US children, 18.9% had allergic rhinitus and 5.8% had a food allergy (ACAAI, 2024).

## Why some people become allergic and others don't

How come two people can breathe the same pollen but only one sneezes irritably? Western medicine proposes a few hypotheses, but none of them are proven.

One possible reason is heredity. A child with one allergic parent is clearly more likely to be allergic, and with two allergic parents the odds increase. Specific genes are known. Mutations in filaggrin, a protein that holds the skin barrier together, raise the risk of atopic dermatitis (eczema), and genome-wide studies also point to genes for IL-33, TSLP, IL13 and STAT6, all of which sit on the Th2 pathway from the last section.

The second possible reason is the barrier. If the lining of your skin, nose or gut leaks, allergens get to immune cells sooner and in worse company. The epithelial barrier hypothesis proposes that modern detergents, pollutants and processed food damage these linings and push the immune system toward allergy (Akdis, 2021). This is just a hypothesis, though.

The third possible reason is the hygiene hypothesis. It says that children raised around animals and a variety of microbes are less likely to develop asthma and allergy. In a study of European farm children, exposure to a wider range of barnyard microbes was linked with lower asthma risk (Ege et al., 2011). This is just a correlation, not necessarily a causation. The microbiome (the community of bacteria living in and on you) is the likely mediator, though the mechanism is not proven. Antibiotics in infancy are a related suspect. A meta-analysis found early-life antibiotic exposure linked to higher risk of reported hay fever (odds ratio 1.23), eczema (1.26) and food allergy (1.42). However, it's interesting that while life outcomes improved, they did not respond better to objective markers such as skin-prick tests or IgE levels (Ahmadizar et al., 2018).

The fourth answer is the timing of first exposure. The LEAP trial tested the second half: infants at high risk of peanut allergy who ate peanut early became much less likely to be allergic by age five than those who avoided it (LEAP Study, n.d.).

The explanation lies at multiple levels: genes set the starting odds, the barrier decides how much allergen gets in, and early exposure teaches the immune system whether to tolerate it. Nature and nurture interplay.

## Treatment in modern medicine

Because modern medicine maps allergy to specific steps, each drug targets a specific step. Antihistamines, such as over-the-counter Zyrtec, work at the last link of the chain. Histamine has to dock onto the H1 receptor to cause itching and swelling, and most antihistamines are inverse agonists, meaning they bind to that receptor and pushes it below its baseline activity (Church & Church, 2011). Corticosteroids, such as in the form of nasal sprays, work upstream of that, decreasing the broader inflammation that mast-cell mediators set off. They bind the glucocorticoid receptor, which then switches off inflammatory switches like NF-κB inside the cell (Reichardt et al., 2021). Epinephrine is the emergency drug for anaphylaxis, the whole-body reaction in which blood pressure drops and airways close, because it reverses the effects. (Kemp et al., 2008). Anti-IgE antibody therapy goes further back and removes the IgE that arms the mast cells in the first place.

All of these manage the reaction of the immune system. The one treatment that tries to teach the immune system is allergen immunotherapy, which gives you the allergen itself in small, increasing doses, as shots or under-the-tongue tablets. Over time IgE levels gradually fall and IgG4, a different antibody that can mop up allergen without triggering mast cells, rises. The T cell balance shifts away from Th2, and regulatory cells (Tregs and Tr1 cells, the immune system's brakes) become more active (Akdis & Akdis, 2015). It is slow, usually years of treatment, and the benefit tends to last only if treatment is continued long enough. For peanut immunotherapy, reactions and relapse after stopping are documented problems (Carnazza et al., 2025).

## Treatment in TCM

If allergy is a weak border plus a lingering pathogen, treatment focuses on clearing what is stuck at the gate, and strengthening the city guards. Acupuncture does this through points on the Lung and Large Intestine channels and on the back and face. Acupuncture manuals list BL-12 (Fengmen) on the upper back behind the lungs, as "especially suitable in the treatment of such cases of perennial and seasonal allergic rhinitis" (Deadman et al., 2007, p. 266), BL-2 for hay fever (p. 257), and LI-20 beside the nostril for rhinitis that includes sneezing, discharge and itching (p. 120). "Allergies that arise in the spring are treated in the same season to achieve the greatest results" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 6).

Herbs address the same pattern from the inside and are chosen to match the symptoms: for a patient named Carlos, herbs "that cleared Wind, Heat, and Dampness from the head and sinuses while moisturizing and decongesting the Qi of the Lung, Stomach, and Intestines" brought his symptoms under control without the sleepiness he had on antihistamines (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 14), and for Dan's food reactions, Spleen-tonifying herbs were taken before meals "to improve his assimilation, help him last longer between meals, and lessen his allergic reactions" (Ch. 8). Both are single case stories with no comparison group, so they show how the reasoning works without testing whether it does.

Herbal medicine is another tool acupuncturists use to strengthen the body. For example, herbs that clear Wind, Heat, and Dampness (which iare excess pathogens that invaded the body's defenses) can bring allergy symptoms under control. Or, herbs that tonify, or support the spleen, can help select individuals with food allergies. However, herbal prescriptions, just like acupuncture treatment, is highly dependent on the context of the individual, so every treatment plan may look different.

What do the clinical studies say about if acupuncture is empirically proven to be effective for allergies? The best-known German trial randomized 422 people with hay fever to real acupuncture, sham acupuncture (needles in the wrong places or shallow) or only antihistamines. At eight weeks the real acupuncture group scored about 0.5 points better on a quality-of-life scale than sham and about 0.7 better than medication alone, but the authors cautioned the difference may not be clinically significant and it did not hold at week 16 (Brinkhaus et al., 2013). In other words, the needles seemed to do something, but the results were to small to be sure.

A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 30 trials with 4,413 participants. It found acupuncture improved nasal symptoms and quality of life in adults compared with no treatment, and beat sham acupuncture on a nasal symptom scale (mean difference −0.60, 95% CI −1.16 to −0.04). It found no clear difference between acupuncture and the antihistamines cetirizine or loratadine (He et al., 2022). That sounds like a win, but the authors listed explained caution. Trial sequential analysis, a test of whether the pooled result is large and stable enough to trust, failed to confirm the findings. Performance bias and attrition bias were serious in most trials, because patients can usually tell whether a needle was real and many dropped out. The data for children was too little to say anything (He et al., 2022).

An important limitation is that acupuncture ranking higher than "no treatment" can be biased by expectation and the attention received from the acpuncturist, which is why sham comparisons matter, and the sham comparison is where the improvements shrinks. The summary is that acupuncture may modestly help adults with allergic rhinitis, possibly about as much as an antihistamine, and that the evidence is not yet strong enough to be sure.

## Comparing the two maps

The similarities are real. Both maps place the trouble at the body's boundary: the skin, nose, airway and gut that face the outside world. Both treat the trigger as necessary but not sufficient, since the same pollen is harmless to most people. Both hold that history shapes who becomes allergic. TCM blames repeated antibiotics for weakening digestive Fire (the Spleen and Stomach's warming, digesting function) and, through it, the body's defences (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 5). Modern data show a modest echo of that idea: early-life antibiotics are linked to more parent-reported hay fever, eczema and food allergy, though not to objective skin-prick or IgE results (Ahmadizar et al., 2018). The grouping of tissues overlaps too. TCM says pathogens invade the skin, nose and airway when the Lung's defence is weak, and the epithelial barrier hypothesis says that damage to the linings of the skin, airways and gut lets allergens in and drives allergy (Akdis, 2021).

The differences are just as real, and they set the treatment. Modern medicine asks which step failed and gives a drug for that step, so the same antihistamine suits every patient with the same reaction, and the question "did it work" can be answered with a placebo comparison. TCM asks which pattern the patient is in, so two people with the same sneeze can get different points or herbs, and the person, not the symptom, is what gets treated. The first approach is easy to empirically prove that a drug works as treatment, it's easy to diagnose a patient with specific illness and assign a drug. The TCM approach tailors to the person as a whole in the context of their life and is hard to prove, which is why the trials above struggle to separate real needling from sham. Neither difference makes one map the right one. They answer different questions, one about mechanism and one about the whole patient over time.

Modern medicine zooms in to the mast cell, TCM zooms out to the whole person and season. Both look at the same body with two maps at two magnifying levels. Both maps put allergy at the body's boundary. The epithelial barrier hypothesis and the Lung-governs-skin-and-nose idea describe a defended surface from two directions.

**Each map has a blind spot the other covers.** The molecular map explains the mechanism but gives little guidance about why this person, this spring. The TCM map speaks to the person but cannot show the mechanism and offers weaker evidence. A reader who holds both asks better questions of either.

As for treatment, western medicine covers the symptoms while acupuncture and herbal medicine address the problem at the root, although there is a lack of empirical studies demonstrating this efficacy.
