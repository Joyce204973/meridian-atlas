The morning we were supposed to fly to Jeju, one of my three friends texted that she had spent the night in the hospital and might miss the flight.

She made it to the gate just before boarding, swallowed a painkiller, and told us what happened. She had been walking outside our dorm in Seoul when she nearly blacked out and barely made it back to her room. This happens to her every few months, around her period. The hospital ran CT scans and blood tests, the standard workup. Everything came back normal, so they let her go just like that.

She laughed it off. "At least I know nothing is wrong with me."

My stomach sank. Because she thought her normal test results meant that she was healthy and that this monthly struggle was normal. The power of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) is that we can spot problems before they manifest into full blown diseases because we look at the system and see where it is out of balance. A scan or blood test quanitifying specific variables cannot capture imbalance in a system.

## What the Western Map Says

Most period pain has a name: primary dysmenorrhea, meaning cramps with no structural cause found. The mechanism is understood. Women with dysmenorrhea have high levels of prostaglandins, hormones that make the uterus contract and cause cramping abdominal pain.

Every month the female uterus builds a thick lining to support a possible pregnancy. If no egg is fertilized, progesterone, the hormone holding that lining in place, drops fast, and the lining breaks down and sheds. That’s the period. As the lining breaks down, enzymes inside the dying cells free up a fatty acid called arachidonic acid. Another enzyme, COX, turns it into prostaglandins.

Prostaglandins are messenger molecules that tell the uterine muscle: contract and the blood vessels in the uterine wall to narrow. Muscle short on oxygen builds up waste products, and the nearby pain nerves pick up on them, which is felt as a cramp. Women with period pain have higher prostaglandin levels than women without (Itani et al., 2022, p. 103). Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs block cyclooxygenase (COX), the enzyme that makes prostaglandins. With less prostaglandin, the squeezing eases and the vessels open back up

Thus, females struggling with menstrual cramps are often recommended to turn to the first-line of therapies recommended for treating menstrual cramps: NSAIDs and hormonal contraceptives, since they inhibit the production of prostaglandins, that are directly correlated to menstrual pain and its associated systemic symptoms.  In a meta-review that pooled 80 randomized trials and 5,820 women, they found that the odds of moderate or excellent pain relief were about 4.4 times higher on NSAIDs than on placebo. In plain numbers: if 18% of women on placebo get relief, roughly 45–53% on an NSAID do (Marjoribanks et al., 2015).

However, ibuprofen and hormonal birth control are both simply bandaids for a problem that western medicine can’t solve. This is where TCM offers insightful wisdom, where acupuncture and herbal medicine can even alleviate all these symptoms.

## What the TCM Map Says

Chinese medicine begins from a different question. Not “*What is wrong?”* but rather *“what is out of harmony?”*

Beinfield and Korngold highlight the strength of the TCM system is "its power to detect and meaningfully describe dysfunction even though the structure appears intact and physically sound" (1991, ch. 3).

### What Your Period Can Tell You

In Chinese medicine, your period works like a monthly report on the rest of your body. A practitioner pays attention to when it comes, how much there is, what the blood looks like, and whether it hurts. Together these show how well the Liver, Spleen, Kidneys, and uterus are doing their jobs. They also show whether the body’s supply of Qi, its working energy, and Blood, the nourishing fluid that feeds the body, is full or running low (Chen, 2011, pp. 217–218 of PDF).

#### A healthy period

A healthy cycle lasts about 28 days, and the bleeding lasts 3 to 5 days. The blood is bright red, neither watery nor sticky, and may contain small, simple clots (Chen, 2011, p. 217 of PDF). Western medicine commonly cites a wider range of 21 to 35 days for an adult cycle, so a cycle can look fine on one map and still raise a question on the other.

#### Timing

A period that arrives more than a week early is usually explained in one of two ways. Either the body’s working energy is run down, which Chinese medicine calls Qi deficiency, or there’s too much heat, which it calls Blood heat (Chen, 2011, p. 218 of PDF).

Why does a period arrive a week or more late? The first is a supply problem, which Chinese medicine calls deficiency. The body may have too little nourishing Blood to build the period on time, or its Yang may be weak. Yang is the warming, active side of the body, so weak Yang works like a furnace turned too low. The second is a traffic problem, which Chinese medicine calls excess. Qi can get stuck, and when energy stops flowing (Qi stagnation) the Blood behind it stalls too, slowing to a standstill in one spot (Blood stasis). Cold can also thicken the Blood the way cooking oil turns sluggish in a cold pan, which is what the term “congealing” describes (Chen, 2011, p. 218 of PDF).

Why does a period come early one month and late the next? There are two possible reasons. The supply version comes from a worn-down Spleen and Kidneys, the organs Chinese medicine ties to energy and deep reserves. The traffic version comes from a Liver that is not keeping Qi flowing smoothly, or from Blood that has stalled (Chen, 2011, p. 218 of PDF). Excess heat is one cause of an early period, and excess cold is one cause of a late one.

#### Amount

What does it mean for a period to be short or light? On the supply side, there may be too little nourishing Blood, or the Kidney Qi may be weak. On the traffic side, cold may be thickening the Blood, the Blood may be stuck, or Phlegm-Damp may be blocking the flow. Phlegm-Damp is the Chinese medicine term for a heavy, sticky buildup of fluid that clogs the body’s channels(Chen, 2011, p. 218 of PDF).

A noticeably heavy period is explained by Blood heat, Qi deficiency, or Phlegm-Damp (Chen, 2011, p. 219 of PDF). Imagine Qi as the thing that holds Blood in its channels. When Qi is too weak to hold the Blood in, the Blood leaks out faster than it should (Chen, 2011, p. 368 of PDF).

#### Color and texture

Pale, thin blood points to too little Blood to go around. Brownish, thick blood suggests strong Heat in the Blood. Purplish blood with clots points to cold thickening the Blood and leaving it stuck (Chen, 2011, p. 219 of PDF).

#### Period pain

Painful periods, called dysmenorrhoea, mean pain in the lower abdomen during the period or just before or after it. Chinese medicine usually traces it to one of five causes: stuck Qi, stuck Blood, cold thickening the Blood, weak Yang, or too little Qi and Blood. A practitioner tells them apart by asking what the pain feels like and what else is going on in the body (Chen, 2011, p. 219 of PDF).

A feeling of pressure and swelling accompanying the pain points to stuck Qi. Sharp pain, like being stabbed with a needle, points to stuck Blood. Pain that feels cold and eases with warmth points to cold or weak Yang. Pain that is bearable but steady points to a supply problem (Chen, 2011, pp. 188–189 of PDF).

In this map, period pain is a signal of imbalance, which is different depending on the individual.

**Stagnation.** The Liver stores Blood and releases it. When that goes smoothly, a period flows smoothly. When it doesn't, " the uterus will cramp" (1991, p. 107). There may also be tension, irritability, and breast tenderness before a period.

**Blood stasis.** If stagnation persists, it can harden into something more fixed. Obstructed Blood causes "severe menstrual cramps with dark blood or clots," "persistent localized, stabbing, or throbbing pains," and "pain worse from pressure or massage" (1991, ch. 14). This manifests as pain that is sharp, in one place, and worse when pressed.

**Cold.** "Cold depresses metabolism and retards circulation" (1991, p. 94). Cold can arrive from outside, but also from within, from low metabolic fire (Yang) or from eating a lot of what the book calls Cold things: "Refrigerated or raw foods and chilled drinks are Cold. Ice cream and iced drinks are particularly damaging to the Yang energy" (1991, ch. 5). Cold thickens things and slows them down, including Blood. That’s why my Chinese mom has always been adamant that I do not consume any cold foods or drinks the week leading up to my period.

**Deficiency.** Not every painful period is too much. Some are too little. Symptoms of deficient Blood is dizziness, fatigue, dry skin, pale lips and nails (1991, ch. 6). A body that has less to work with can cramp and drain at the same time. This pattern looks the opposite of stasis: duller, tired, better with rest.

## The Wisdom of Both Maps

TCM asks more about our menstrual cycles than western medicine asks. Is it sharp and fixed, or dull and spread out? Does pressure make it worse, or does a hot water bottle make it better? Are there dark clots? Do your hands and feet run cold? Do you get lightheaded, tired, pale? What is the week before like? What do you usually eat?

Menstrual pain is often normalized in our culture. Many women are taught that bad periods are just part of having a uterus. The Chinese one sees pain as information on how to support the body.

Warmth is a low-risk place to start noticing. A practitioner might suggest paying attention to cold drinks and raw food in the days before a period, and to warm food and a warm lower abdomen during it.

My experience:

Ever since I got my menstrual cycle, I managed my monthly debilitating pain with ibuprofen. After a decade, the symptoms grew into migraines, lethargy, lightheadedness, and hot sweats that crippled me for one week a month. I receive acupuncture weekly for other health problems, but very quickly, my menstrual cramps–and every other debilitating symptom–vanished. This is not evidence but just my personal experience.

 The research on acupuncture for period pain is encouraging but weak. A 2018 meta-analysis of 60 trials, 55 of them in China, found acupuncture reduced pain more than NSAIDs (SMD −0.63, 95% CI −0.88 to −0.37), but the authors describe the studies as low quality, blinding was difficult, and in the placebo-controlled trials only one of three showed a significant benefit (Woo et al., 2018).
