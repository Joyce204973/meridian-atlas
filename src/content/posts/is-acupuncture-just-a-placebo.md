When I tell people I want to study Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), somebody usually asks, “isn’t acupuncture just a placebo?” I believe that acupuncture and western medicine are based on two completely different maps of the body, with acupuncture being based on thousands of years of pattern observation and western medicine rooted in the scientific method. While one map of the body cannot do justice to explaining a different, more traditional map of the body, we can use empirical studies to strengthen the mechanisms that acupuncture works beyond a mere placebo effect.

 I want to work through it by zooming in from the person lying on the table down to the tip of one needle and then back out again, and at each level to set what the Western studies say beside what the Chinese tradition says, since they are each answering different questions about the same body.

## Starting with the patient

Researchers pooled the raw data from 39 randomized trials, 20,827 people in all, who had chronic pain in the back or neck, the knee or hip, the shoulder, or the head (Vickers et al., 2018). Within each trial, people had been randomly assigned to real acupuncture, to a sham version built to feel like acupuncture (sometimes needles that touch the skin without piercing it, sometimes needles placed at spots that are not acupuncture points), or to no acupuncture at all. From their statistical analyses, an interpretation of their results would be: out of 100 people those who felt their pain fall by half or more would be around 30 with no acupuncture, around 42 with sham, and around 50 with real acupuncture. Acupuncture moderately improves the pain more than the other groups.

Because the fake acupuncture showed a moderate improvement, there is a lot of room for the placebo effect. More bias clouds the results when we consider that the people in the no-acupuncture groups knew they were not being treated, the practitioners knew which needles were real, and pain is something people report themselves.

## The needle

So what does a needle do once it is in?

One answer of a structural response comes from the Western side comes from a mouse experiment by Liu and colleagues (2021), who asked why a point on the hind leg, ST36, calms inflammation while the same current at a point on the belly, ST25, does not. They engineered mice so that one family of sensory nerve cells glowed red under the microscope, and they found those fibers packed into the deep connective tissue around the leg bones, about ten times denser at ST36 than at the belly point. To check that the nerves actually caused the effect, they gave mice a bacterial toxin at a dose that kills about eighty percent of the nerves and passed a gentle current through needles at ST36, which cut two inflammation signals roughly in half and raised survival by forty percent (liu, 2021). These results support that acupuncture plays a role in decreasing inflammation. It’s important to note that this study was done in male mice and the outcome was inflammation and not pain.  Chinese medicine would describe the same point tested in the study with a different system, as a place on the Stomach channel, one of the pathways said to link skin, muscle, tendon and bone to the organs and to bind the body into a single whole (Deadman et al., 2001, p. 11).

Another mechanism is in the tissue. After the needle is twisted, the patient feels a dull, heavy ache spread out from it, while the practitioner feels the tissue seem to grip the needle, a tug like a fish biting a fishing line. Chinese medicine calls this De Qi, the arrival of Qi(Langevin et al., 2001). Helene Langevin and her colleagues wanted to know what the tug physically is. When they twisted needles 32 times in pieces of rat abdominal wall and preserved the tissue a minute later, collagen fibers had wound around the shaft like thread on a spool, and in human volunteers it took more force to pull a needle out after twisting than after plain insertion. That fits the idea that the tissue grips the needle and that the pull then travels outward through the connective tissue to the cells living in it. The rat tissue had been removed from the animal, so we can only cautiously assume the effects for people.

Another possible mechanism lies in the myofascia that crosses the body. A classic acupuncture technique is to needle the opposite side to treat the problem, known as distal points. Needling the arms to treat the legs and the left side to treat the right side. One anatomical idea that fits is Thomas Myers's description of lines of muscle and connective tissue running through the body, including two that cross the trunk in an X in the front and the back of the body and a double helix that wraps around. These three patterns of connective tissue connect each arm to the opposite leg (Myers, 2020). While acupuncturists would say needling points far away from the intended location of treatment is effective because blood flow and qi are circulating, the myofascia system that connects the body’s muscles and connective tissue can offer a plausible mechanism.

These three sources provide plausible mechanisms that acupuncture affects more than just the mind, even if we can’t quite see the anatomical structures.

Other proposed theories are that acupuncture minimizes pain through modifying the transmission of neural impulses between the spinal cord and the brain. This blocks pain fibers. It also triggers endorphins, serotonin, and dopamine. (Kaiser Permanente, 2026 & Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 305). Other biologists suggest that the body is an electric field of electrical conductors, which form the meridians. Readings found that each acupuncture point had an electrical field surrounding it with its own shape (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 308).

## The Body As A System in TCM

Beinfield and Korngold call the Western approach the mechanic's view: "the body becomes an electronic and biochemical network of transmitting and receiving stations with messages flowing back and forth along specific single-function pathways" (1991, p. 241). The scientific method is empirically proven and rigorous. Yet, it rests on the foundation of a reductionist method, dissecting the problem into parts and missing the whole. “Because of such a narrow focus, the possibility of multiple and simultaneous interactions is overlooked."

## Chinese Medicine Is Chinese Philosophy

Chinese medicine has been around for over 2,000 years built on pattern observation (Gaur, 2024). It is more than a physical treatment for pain. It is a philosophy applied to the body, which means it is unscientific, when measured in the modern sense.

Western medicine advances by asking *“How does it work*” so that we can manipulate processes and change lives for the better. Chinese Medicine is less concerned with why acupuncture works but *how and when to use it.* Where Western medicine asks *what is broken?*, Chinese medicine asks *what is the body trying to say?* Illness is not just malfunction. It is a signal about where the circulation has stagnated. The body speaks in patterns, and the practitioner balances it before the disbalance results in illness.

The most powerful treatments do not stop at moving Qi through the channels. They treat the Shen — the spirit, the mind-heart that the Chinese language does not separate into two words the way English does. A shift in how a person feels about themselves can change the course of their condition. Not because the illness was imagined. Because the spirit and the body are united (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991). For example, in the Chinese language, a “psychological” problem is called, in direct translation, a problem in the “heart.”

This is where the so-called placebo effect gets interesting. What if the "placebo" is simply what happens when a person becomes willing to change — and the body, which was waiting for that willingness, finally moves? You have to be willing to change in order for change to occur. Understandably so, the placebo effect is a weakness in western empirical studies because it means the effect is not coming from the drug but coming from you. While people skeptically call acupuncture a “placebo,” the truth is that it doesn’t matter if the result came from the needle or your mind, if you improved, for you are your greatest healer (Yuan, J., lecture notes).

## The Current Inside You

Qi and Blood move through the body along a network of channels and collaterals — Jing (經) and Luo (絡).

Jing translates to "channels," also called meridians — a word that carries the geography it implies. Rivers running through a landscape. These are the deep trunks that flow longitudinally through the body: twelve primary channels, eight extraordinary vessels, twelve divergent channels. Each primary channel connects to a zangfu — an organ system — and each is responsible for certain characteristics of how the body functions (Yuan, J., lecture notes).

Luo means "a net." Thinner branches — fifteen luo-connecting channels that interweave the trunks, plus finer collaterals down to the minute level, reaching every tissue.

Together, the channels penetrate the zangfu at the deepest level and connect outward to skin, muscles, flesh, tendons, bones, limbs, head, and sense organs, linking every structure into one integrated whole (Deadman et al., 2001, p. 11).

Imagine a tree. The zangfu are the roots. The channels are the trunk and branches. The body's tissues and sense organs are the flowers that open to the world. The Heart flowers into the tongue. The Liver opens into the eyes. Pull a root and the flower wilts. Tend the root or trunk and the flower opens.

Western medicine maps the body into separate systems — cardiovascular, digestive, respiratory, nervous, musculoskeletal, endocrine, lymphatic, urinary, and reproductive. Each with its own specialists, its own textbooks, its own floor of the hospital. Gratefully, medicine has become so powerful and specialized, but this specialization comes at a cost.

TCM asks a different question: what connects them? The answer is the channel network. The Qi and Blood flowing through it are what make the body one thing rather than a collection of parts. The acupuncturist is not a mechanic hunting for a broken valve. "The acupuncturist is a practical ecologist who never loses sight of the whole while attending to any of the parts" — more shepherd than surgeon, "diverting river water into terraced fields in an attempt to orchestrate a fortuitous convergence of natural forces" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 242).

How did anyone discover invisible channels? Two theories evolved:

The first says that over centuries, people noticed pressing tender spots on the body relieved symptoms elsewhere. Massage and heat at certain locations produced consistent effects. Points were found through observation — then grouped into channels based on shared characteristics. Points first, channels second (Deadman et al., 2007, p. 12).

The second says that practitioners exploring the body's internal landscape through meditation and qigong felt the channels directly — currents of sensation flowing along specific pathways. Channels first, points second (Deadman et al., 2007, p. 12).

The network does two things: it nourishes every layer of the body, and it defends it. The channels form a series of barriers from the surface level to the deepest— skin, then minute collaterals, then luo-connecting channels, then primary channels, then the five zang organs. Treatment at the surface catches a pathogen before it can reach deeper (Deadman et al., 2007, p. 15). This is how Chinese medicine is preventative rather than focused on treating symptoms – strengthening the flow of Qi and Blood through the meridians is akin to strengthening the fortress that keeps invaders out before they’ve even penetrated through.

## Two maps to view the whole body

This is where the two maps begin to look like two lenses on the same body, since nerves and collagen are what you see at the scale of a needle, and the idea that Qi and Blood run through the body in channels were observed thousands of years ago.

Western science traces a chain: needle touches fascia, collagen grips, nerve fibers fire, the brain quiets. It is precise. It does not explain why the patient weeps on the table and walks out lighter.

Chinese medicine sees a gate opening into a river system, stagnant Qi stirred back into motion, the body given a clear path to do what it already knows how to do. It accounts for the whole.

"In Western medicine a given drug or procedure results in a limited, desired effect, plus so-called side effects. In Chinese medicine, this logic is reversed, so that global changes in the whole organism result in the disappearance of specific symptoms" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 242).

The needle enters the body and the body responds in the fascia and in the spirit, in the connective tissue and in the channels, everywhere all at once the system adjusts. It pains me to hear people diminish acupuncture to be *good at pain relief* because the pain is just the weed of a much deeper root. Acupuncture finds those roots and tends to it to clean the entire garden. Acupuncturists do not just erase the pain and neglect the disbalance in the five elements and meridians, or the patient’s emotional state, or the physiological function at another point. That is a disgrace to a powerful art. A good acupuncturist seeks to balance the body as a whole, and as a consequence, the original symptom subsides or disappears.

I am not a practitioner yet. I am a biology student who has sat in clinics across San Francisco, Taipei, Shanghai, Seoul, Tokyo, and Buenos Aires learning from practitioners around the world. I do not have all the answers. But I know enough to tell you that you can not hold the western reductionist map of the body to say "acupuncture is just the placebo effect.”

The better question is not *does it work*? It is *what happens when you look from another perspective?*
