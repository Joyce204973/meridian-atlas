My biohacker role model on social media swears by her signature brunch appetizer: a raw carrot salad drizzled with olive oil and lemon juice. Carrots are packed with beta-carotene. Great for glowing skin. Clean, whole, unprocessed. I was inspired.

So I made the same carrot salad as my vegetable dish and brought it to my internship with my acupuncturist. He took one look at my lunch and his face changed.

"You're eating *that?*"

I didn't understand. It was a salad. Vegetables. Fiber. How could a carrot salad possibly be wrong?

## The Case for Salads

Let me be fair to the salad. It deserves it.

Raw vegetables give you fiber and micronutrients while keeping you feeling light. Fiber is a prebiotic — gut bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, propionate, and acetate, which maintain the gut lining, lower inflammation, and protect against chronic disease (Tan et al., 2014; Blaak et al., 2020). Raw produce also carries soil microbes that can increase gut biodiversity and strengthen innate immunity (Lang et al., 2023). Epidemiologically, higher fruit and vegetable intake is consistently associated with reduced risk of colorectal cancer, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and type 2 diabetes.

Raw vegetables retain heat-sensitive vitamins — vitamin C, certain B vitamins — that cooking degrades. And there's something undeniably appealing about the simplicity: whole foods in their purest, most unprocessed form.

So how could you possibly go wrong with a salad?

## The Part Nobody Talks About

Here's what's quietly true for some people: salads make them bloated. They eat a clean lunch, feel heavy and gassy by 3pm, and assume the problem is them rather than the food. They push through it because they believe raw is healthy.

Western science has some things to say about why this happens.

**Your body absorbs less from raw vegetables than you'd think.** That raw carrot salad I was so proud of? A study on beta-carotene bioavailability found that absorption was over two times greater from cooked carrots than from raw ones (Livny et al., 2003). Heat breaks down plant cell walls and releases the carotenoids trapped inside. The nutrient is there in the raw carrot — your body just can't get to it as efficiently. Similarly, heating tomatoes for 30 minutes increases available lycopene by 164% compared to raw (Dewanto et al., 2002). Fat-soluble nutrients — vitamins A, D, E, K, and carotenoids like beta-carotene — absorb significantly better when cooked with fat.

So the influencer's raw carrot salad with olive oil and lemon? She's getting less beta-carotene from that carrot than she would from a simple roasted one.

**Raw fiber can overwhelm weak digestion.** This is the paradox: the very fiber that feeds beneficial bacteria in a healthy gut can wreck a compromised one. Raw vegetables have intact cell walls made of cellulose that are hard to break down. If your digestion isn't strong enough to handle that efficiently, the fiber sits in the colon and ferments — producing gas, distension, bloating, and pain. For people with IBS (Irritable Bowel Syndrome) or FODMAP sensitivity (specific carbohydrates and sugar alcohols that cause digestive issues like bloating and pain because they are poorly absorbed and ferment in the gut), this isn't a minor inconvenience. A meta-analysis found that 75% of IBS patients improved significantly within one week of reducing raw, high-FODMAP foods (Marsh et al., 2016).

The standard gastroenterological advice for patients with digestive struggles is to cook your vegetables.

## What My Acupuncturist Knew

For the majority of people whose digestive systems are strong enough to handle the raw vegetables, this advice does not apply. The beauty of TCM is that every body is unique and deserves a different lifestyle. In Chinese medicine, no food is inherently good or bad. The question is never "is this healthy?" It's "healthy *for whom*, prepared *how*, eaten *when, how much?*"

This is what Beinfield and Korngold call the foundational principle of Chinese food therapy: "Who we are determines what is most beneficial for us to eat... the appropriateness of a food cannot be established without knowledge of the context — not everyone will benefit equally from foods that contain the same measure of nutrients. In other words, 'one man's meat is another man's poison'" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 323).

TCM teaches us that the function of the food in the body changes depending on how you prepare it. Let’s look at the carrot.

The properties of food describe what it does to the body’s energy. A raw carrot is slightly sour, moisturizing, cooling, and dispersing — good for decongesting Qi and Heat in the Liver and Stomach. A cooked carrot becomes sweeter, warming, and consolidating — good for replenishing Qi of the Spleen, Lung, and Kidney (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 327). Same vegetable. Different therapeutic action. The preparation changes what the food *does* inside your body.

My grandma cooks with the function of foods in mind. One of her go-to soups is simple: carrots, corn on the cob with the husks, and pork, simmered for hours. In TCM terms, this soup is brilliant. The carrots, now cooked, are sweet and warming — they tonify Spleen Qi rather than cooling the Liver the way raw carrots would. Corn is sweet and neutral, benefits the Stomach and Spleen, and promotes the drainage of Dampness. Pork is sweet, salty, and cool-to-neutral — it nourishes Yin and moisturizes without generating Heat, and its richness supplements Blood and Essence through the Kidney. The long simmer extracts everything into a warm broth that the Spleen barely has to work to absorb. It's a supplementing, harmonizing meal. Generations of Chinese grandmothers didn't need a nutrition label to know that a warm bowl of soup after a long day does more for you than a cold plate of raw greens.

## Why Raw Food Taxes the Spleen

The Spleen in Chinese medicine is the engine of digestion — not the anatomical organ, but the entire system that transforms food into Qi and Blood. Beinfield and Korngold describe it as "a crucible, a cooking pot, warmed by the living fire from the body's core, the Kidney. The Spleen depends on the ascension of heat from below to convert food to Qi and Blood" (1991, p. 328).

When you eat raw food, that cooking pot has to do double duty: warm the food up *and* break it down. Cooked food arrives pre-transformed — requiring less digestive fire, less work from the Spleen. This is why TCM food therapy has always emphasized cooked, warm meals as the foundation of eating, not because raw food is bad, but because cooked food is easier on the system.

A diet heavy in raw fruits and vegetables cools the body — "not because these foods have been refrigerated, but because they promote the loss of body heat and the secretion of fluid. For a person who is Cold, Damp, and depleted, this diet exaggerates internal climate, aggravating symptoms of chilliness, puffiness, phlegm, fullness, and fatigue" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 324).

But"the same salads and juicy fruits that undermine an already cool, moist person are therapeutic for someone who is hot and dry" (p. 324). It is not one-size-fits-all. A person running hot — red face, irritability, constipation, feels warm all the time — might genuinely benefit from a raw salad for lunch. A person who runs cold — bloating, fatigue, cold hands, loose stools — might be making themselves worse with every virtuous bowl of greens.

My mom figured this out through years of reading. Once she learned that raw fruits and vegetables and cold, sticky dairy products worsen Damp, Cold Spleens, she started teaching me. My favorite yogurts, cheese, cottage cheese, cream cheese, cow's milk, peaches, bananas — all subject to a sharp *tsk*. Dairy products, like fruit, are dampening on the body.

"You complain about your health problems? Your body cannot digest it, so if you eat that, don't complain to me. I can't help you."

She wasn't being dramatic. She was being precise.

I used to eat as much fruit as I wanted in a day because social media influencers championed raw food diets, and fruit silenced my cravings for sweets and chocolate. It felt like a clean trade. But the bloating that slowly worsened – that was the Spleen waving a white flag. I just didn't know how to read it until it got worse.

## Where the Two Maps Meet

Both frameworks arrive at the same conclusion through different reasoning.

Western science says: cooking breaks plant cell walls, increasing bioavailability of beta-carotene, lycopene, and other fat-soluble nutrients. Raw fiber overwhelms weak digestion and causes fermentation-driven bloating. Cook your vegetables if your gut can't handle them raw.

Chinese medicine says: cooking pre-transforms food so the Spleen doesn't have to spend its own fire doing the work. Raw, cold food depletes Spleen Yang and generates Dampness in people who are already Cold and deficient. Cook your food if your digestion is weak.

Same observation. Different language. Neither map is wrong.

The key is that this applies most to people who already have compromised digestion — whether you call it IBS, FODMAP sensitivity, and weak gut motility, or Spleen Qi deficiency with Dampness accumulation. If your digestion is strong, if you run warm, if salads make you feel light and energized — eat the salad. The point isn't that raw food is bad. The point is that raw food isn't universally good, and the person matters as much as the plate.

## What to Notice

Next time you eat a big salad, pay attention to the two hours that follow. Do you feel light and energized? Or heavy and bloated? Do you reach for something warm afterward — tea, soup, something cooked — because your body is asking for it?

If salads consistently leave you feeling worse than before you ate, that's not a personal failing. That's information. Your body is telling you that it needs its food prepared differently — warmer, softer, pre-broken-down. A bowl of roasted vegetables with rice. A warm soup. Sautéed greens instead of a raw pile.

You're still eating the vegetables. You're just letting the stove do some of the work your Spleen was struggling to do alone.
