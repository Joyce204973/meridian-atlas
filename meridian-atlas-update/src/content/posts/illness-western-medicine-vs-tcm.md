When something goes wrong in your body, Western medicine asks: what is the cause? A pathogen. A mutation. A structural failure. Find it and remove it. But what if illness is not always something that invades from outside? Drawing from the wisdom of TCM, if we look at health as a balance in the body, then we can begin to identify imbalances in the body as early signs of illness.

## The Western map: what a pathogen is

In Western medicine, a pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease: a bacterium, a virus, a fungus, a parasite. In 1875, Robert Koch injected anthrax-infected blood into a rabbit's ear. The rabbit died. He isolated bacteria from its lymph nodes, grew them in culture, and showed that those bacteria alone could pass the disease to a new animal. Louis Pasteur, his rival, found that a flask of chicken cholera bacteria left out over the summer injected into chicken protected them from cholera, which formed the basis of vaccination. Their combined work "firmly established the germ theory of disease. Prior to this time ... there was no known biological basis for either the cause of diseases or the efficacy of vaccination" (Greenberg, p. 2).

Germ theory gave medicine a clear focus: isolate the cause.

## The Western map: the immune response

Once you know the enemy, the next question is how the body answers it. Immunology describes two systems that work in sequence.

The innate system is fast and general. In the 1880s, Metchnikoff was studying digestion in starfish larvae when he noticed cells engulfing the splinters he had pushed in. He named them phagocytes, "devouring cells," and called them the first line of defense (Greenberg, p. 3). Innate cells do not recognize one specific microbe. They recognize patterns that many microbes share, using receptors such as the Toll-like receptors, and they respond within hours.

The adaptive system is slow and specific. T cells and B cells recognize one particular antigen, B cells make antibodies against it, and some of these cells remember it for next time, and respond faster and stronger. The link between the two systems is the dendritic cell, which Steinman identified in 1978 (Greenberg, pp. 9-11). It collects fragments of the invader in the tissue, carries them to the lymph node, and presents them to T cells. It is the only cell that can start an antigen-specific T-cell response, so the adaptive system waits on its signal.

The immune system is a regulator, not only a fighter. In 1989, Janeway proposed that innate receptors let the body recognize "infectious non-self" and switch on the adaptive response, and Janeway and Medzhitov confirmed it in 1997 (Greenberg, pp. 11-12). Matzinger then pushed further. The same receptors also respond to proteins released by dying or damaged cells, so the trigger may be danger, not infection itself (Matzinger, 1994).

Inflammation helps the body fight pathogens. A sensor cell in the tissue that detects a trigger releases chemical signals, and those signals change the local blood vessels. The vessels widen, which brings more blood to the area and produces heat and redness. They also become leaky, which lets fluid and immune cells move into the tissue and produces swelling. Inflammation is characterized by heat, pain, swelling, and redness (Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care \[IQWiG\], 2025). Virchow saw inflammation as a pathological process. Metchnikoff saw it as protection. Both were right. Medzhitov's framework explains how: sensor cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells detect a trigger, then release chemokines that recruit effector cells and cytokines that activate them (Medzhitov, 2008). The body moves from homeostasis to stress to inflammation to fight the danger.

When the deviation from homeostasis does not resolve, inflammation becomes chronic. So in Western medicine, illness is not only the microbe. It is the microbe, the response to it, and whether the body can return to baseline.

## The TCM map: why is the body out of balance?

There is a phrase in TCM: "The man is not sick because he has an illness; he has an illness because he is sick" (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, Ch. 3). It means that when the body's defenses are weakened, its resources exhausted, and its balance disrupted, that is what permits illness to take root. The disease is the symptom. The imbalance is the cause.

TCM recognizes external and internal causes of disease (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 326). The external causes are six climatic factors: wind, cold, summer-heat, dampness, dryness, and fire. These are environmental forces, not microbes (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 327). The internal causes include the emotions, and other causes include improper diet and overstrain (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 326). The exposure is only half of the story. There are always pathogens around, whether the six of TCM or viruses, and whether you get sick, and how severe it gets, depends on the internal state: how strong your body's natural defense is.

The body's own side of the ledger is *zheng qi*, its upright, healthy qi. Think of zheng qi as the Great Wall of China. Invaders are always trying to get in, but if the wall is strong enough, the person does not get sick, or the illness is milder and shorter. The textbook says the six climatic factors cause disease in two situations: when the weather turns abnormal at the wrong time, or when zheng qi is insufficient (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 327). Illness is then a struggle between pathogenic qi and zheng qi (Chen, 2011, PDF pp. 316-317). A practitioner reads this resistance in the body itself. A strong, well-built body with moist, lustrous skin signals strong resistance against pathogenic invasion, and a thin, withered one signals weak resistance (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 55).

For what we call a cold, TCM calls an *exterior pattern*. Pathogens enter through the skin, mouth, and nose and settle in the muscles and skin (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 327). Wei qi, the defensive qi, rises to meet them and gets stagnant at the surface, which the text links to fever, while the skin goes unwarmed, which it links to aversion to cold. The lung fails to disperse, giving cough, stuffy nose, and runny nose, and the pulse floats near the surface (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 286, Fig. 8-1). The textbook sorts these into three types: attack of cold (severe chill, slight fever, no sweating), attack of wind (aversion to wind, slight fever, sweating), and attack of wind-heat (severe fever, thirst, sore throat) (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 287, Table 8-2). If an exterior pattern is not resolved, the pathogen can. penetrate deeper and move inward to the zang-fu organs, making the illness more severe (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 287).

## A gardener reads the weather and the soil

My mother is a gardener. She rubs the soil between her fingers to check if it's too dry or too wet, then adjusts her watering. If a cold season comes, she notes there will be less fruit. If the wind picks up, she wraps plastic sheeting around her plants to shield them. She doesn't treat the plant. She reads the environment and responds.

A TCM practitioner does the same thing with your body. Dry lips, cracked knuckles, and brittle nails are signs that a body running dry, either because the climate parched it or because something inside lost the ability to distribute its own moisture. Inflammation signals heat. Sluggishness and chills point to cold. They're observations that the system is out of balance, and it should be the responsibility of the patient and doctor to work to readjust the body's balance the way a gardener would add more fertilizer.

## Where the maps meet

Both maps treat illness as a failure of regulation, not only an attack. In immunology, the body moves from homeostasis to stress to inflammation, and illness grows when the response overshoots or fails to switch off. In TCM, illness is a struggle between zheng qi and a pathogen, and it takes root when the body's balance is lost. Take a patch of skin that is red, hot, and swollen. Immunology reads it as inflammation: sensor cells detected a trigger, and chemokines and cytokines called in effector cells. TCM reads the same patch as heat, and asks what has been feeding it. In my reading, these are two descriptions of one regulatory process at two levels. One names the cells and the signals. The other names a pattern and the conditions around it.

The symptoms line up too. Loss of appetite is a sickness behavior in immunology, part of the response itself. The textbook describes reduced appetite in a new illness as often a protective reaction by zheng qi resisting the pathogen (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 208). Two vocabularies, one observation.

## What the TCM map adds: a role before illness

Where they differ is the level of analysis of the immune response. Western medicine works at the level of cells and molecules, which lets it test claims in trials. Understanding the adaptive immune system led to vaccines, which teach the body what an antigen looks like and build antibodies ahead of time. If you get a cold or flu, medication eases symptoms while the immune response does the work.

TCM starts earlier. It reads the state of the body, the strength of zheng qi and the pull of a pathogen, before an illness has a name. A practitioner feels the pulse, which reflects the strength of zheng qi (Chen, 2011, PDF p. 262), and asks about lifestyle and emotions to find ways to support the patient. This is the strength I want to take from the TCM map: it gives the individual something to notice and act on while there is still time, a role before illness and not only during it. Vaccines also act before illness, but they are given to you, one pathogen at a time. TCM offers something different in kind, a daily, individual vocabulary for noticing drift. I should be clear about the limit. Whether these adjustments change outcomes has not been tested the way a vaccine has, so I offer this as a way of paying attention, not as proof.

## Takeaway

Next time you get sick, or just run down, ask two questions instead of one. What was the exposure? And what was the terrain: what were my sleep, food, and stress doing before it arrived? The first question is the one medicine taught us to ask. The second is the one worth adding. If something is persistent or severe, see a doctor.
