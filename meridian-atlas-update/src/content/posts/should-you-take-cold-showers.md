## 3.1 "Should You Take Cold Showers?"

Cold showers are much older than the current wellness trend–cold water exposure has been used by traditional cultures for a long time. When I studied in Tokyo, the acupuncturist I shadowed told me about his hobby of standing under an icy waterfall, a practice called misogi that samurai used to purify the body and spirit.

Cold showers improve metabolism, decrease inflammation, and increase dopamine. This practice of pushing oneself to the extreme for self-improvement is also a ritual viewed with resilience. In the TCM perspective, choosing cold or warm has little to do with willpower but instead depends on your body constitution, which is the balance of elements in your body, and the season of the year.  Hot baths and hot foot soaks count as therapeutic tools, not pampering. The following sections explain what the research shows about the health benefits of cold water, how those benefits occur, and the Chinese Medicine grain of wisdom on how to apply cold showers to your life.

## Types of Cold Water Exposure

There are many ways to do cold water exposure. At one end sits the cold shower, one you can do every morning in the shower. Further along are ice baths and plunges, which researchers call cold-water immersion, then winter swimming, which adds exercise and fresh air. Finally, there is the Wim Hof Method, which pairs cold with a breathing technique and meditation. Most of the science studies the heavier doses, so we have to be mindful of applying the results of studies to cold showers. In the largest recent review of cold-water immersion, ten of eleven trials used baths and only one used showers (Cain et al., 2025, p. 6), so whenever a headline says that cold exposure does something, it is worth asking which cold, for how long, and in whom.

## The Effects

When the rush of cold water hits your skin, the body senses a stress. Thermoreceptors, the nerve endings in your skin that sense temperature, alert the brain, which switches on the sympathetic nervous system, the fight-or-flight branch of the autonomic nervous system that steers the things you do not consciously control, like your heartbeat and breathing. Your heart rate and breathing climb, and your body releases norepinephrine, a messenger that increases attention, and cortisol, the hormone that mobilizes energy under stress (Cain et al., 2025, p. 2). That jolt is the alertness people chase in cold showers.

In a classic experiment, volunteers sat in water up to their necks for an hour at 14°C, and their norepinephrine rose by 530%, their dopamine (the messenger tied to motivation and reward) by 250%, and their metabolic rate (the energy the body burns just to keep going) by 350%. At a milder 20°C the metabolic rate almost doubled, and at a neutral 32°C it did not change (Šrámek et al., 2000, as cited in Mooventhan & Nivethitha, 2014, pp. 199–200). Before you imagine your two-minute shower doing the same, keep two caveats in mind. An hour at 14°C is a far bigger dose than a shower.

Winter swimmers report feeling more vigorous, less tense and less fatigued (Mooventhan & Nivethitha, 2014, p. 200), but winter swimming mixes cold with exercise, open air and company, and a shower can claim only a fraction of that credit.

The controlled trials are more modest and more interesting. When Cain et al. ## Does cold improve your immune system?

In a 2024 randomized controlled trial (volunteers are assigned to groups by chance), 60 healthy adults in Egypt took either a cold or a hot shower every day for 90 days while researchers tracked their blood (El-Ansary et al., 2024). By day 90 the cold group had higher levels of immunoglobulins, the antibodies that latch onto specific germs, and of two cytokines, the chemical messages immune cells send each other: interleukin-2 (IL-2), which tells T cells, the immune cells that kill infected cells, to multiply, and interleukin-4 (IL-4), which helps B cells make antibodies. In the hot shower group, IgM, the first antibody your body makes against a new germ, actually fell. The trial also counted molecules in blood and not clinical symptoms of whether people got sick and their symptoms, and it is a single study of 60 people.

The second is the largest shower trial to date. In it, 3,018 adults took cold showers of 30, 60 or 90 seconds every day for 30 days and had 29% less sickness absence from work than people who showered as usual(Buijze et al., 2016, as cited in Cain et al., 2025, p. 10). The numbers were self-reported, so it is possible that cold showers prevented some infections, and equally possible that people who began their day with a cold shower simply felt well enough to show up. A single cold dip does not seem to do much by itself, because studies that measured immune cells and saliva antibodies right after immersion found no clear change (Cain et al., 2025, p. 8). If cold helps the immune system, it looks like something that builds with repetition and not on the spot, as inflammation markers rose in the first hour after immersion in two small studies (p. 8), perhaps because the body releases stored sugar for fuel (p. 12).

## Wim Hof Method

The most dramatic immune result comes from the Wim Hof Method, which pairs cold exposure with a breathing technique (rounds of deep, rapid breaths followed by breath holds) and meditation. In a 2014 Dutch experiment, 24 healthy young men were injected with endotoxin, a fragment of E. coli bacteria that makes the immune system react as if it were fighting an infection, producing fever, headache and flu-like aches without a real germ. Half of them had spent ten days training with Wim Hof's instructors, which included standing barefoot in snow and swimming in ice-cold water, and they practiced Wim Hof breathing during the experiment. Their immune response looked remarkably calm. Their levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-8, the inflammatory alarm signals that cause fever and aches, were roughly half those of the untrained men, their level of IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine that acts like a brake, was nearly three times higher, and their flu-like symptoms were 56% lower (Kox et al., 2014, p. 7380). The link ran through epinephrine (adrenaline): the more of it a man released during the breathing, the more IL-10 he made (p. 7381).

We cannot say if the results came from the breathing of the cold since both techniques are put together. The technique lowered the carbon dioxide in their blood, made it more alkaline, and briefly cut their oxygen saturation to about half (pp. 7380–7381), and the authors conclude that the breathing, more than the cold, most likely triggered the adrenaline surge (p. 7382). The study cannot say which part of the training mattered, it involved only young men, and it measured a one-time response to a laboratory immune challenge and not real-life illness. A cold shower is one fraction of the Wim Hof Method.

Current research describes cold as a controlled physiological stress to induce benefits over the long term. Most volunteers were young, healthy men, only one of the eleven trials in the main review included women (Cain et al., 2025, p. 13), exposures ranged from 30 seconds to two hours in water between 7°C and 15°C (p. 6), and most results were measured as blood marker or a self-reported score. Thus, western research shows that cold showers possibly offer many health benefits.  But before you push your body to the extreme through sheer discipline, the TCM map reminds us to check in with our bodies.

## What Chinese medicine says

Western research asks whether cold works for the average person. Chinese medicine asks about you and your body. TCM does not care how much cold you can tolerate, but rather, whether your body is in a state to.

The framework starts with yin and yang. Cold belongs to yin, heat belongs to yang, and health is the body's ability to move fluidly between the two. A brief, controlled shock of cold pulls your energy inward, and a body with plenty of yang answers by sending warmth back out, widening the blood vessels and raising circulation. Repeated gently, that exchange trains how well your pores open and close and how freely Qi and Blood travel between the surface and the center of the body.

But in TCM too much cold that overwhelms the body becomes one of the six pathogens, the environmental forces that can invade the body's defenses. Cold slows and tightens: just as cold hands clench and cold muscles cramp, it constricts the vessels and slows the flow of Qi and Blood (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991, p. 94). Warmth drives circulation, digestion and immunity. For someone with plenty of yang fire, a minor stress of a cold shower strengthens their body. For someone with a weak yang fire, cold water is closer to a bucket thrown on a fizzling campfire that was already dying.

A weak fire has a recognizable signature. When Kidney yang, the root of your vitality, runs low, the signs include a weak lower back and knees, frequent urination, loose stools, low libido and difficulty conceiving. When Spleen yang, which digests food and turns it into energy, is weak, the picture is bloating, diarrhea and a heavy, cold, damp feeling after meals. These are the people who struggle to get up in the morning, crave sunlight, and tell themselves that it is supposed to be hard and they just need to tough it out. In TCM theory, cold that the body cannot push back can also make Blood and Moisture congeal into clots or lumps, and stagnation is where illness takes root (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991).

## One temperature, two bodies

The two maps agree that a cold shower costs the body something, and they differ in the question that follows. The Western papers describe a sympathetic stress response in which the metabolic rate climbs by 93% at 20°C and by 350% at 14°C as the body burns more fuel to hold its core temperature (Mooventhan & Nivethitha, 2014, p. 200). This could be seen as good for weight loss. TCM asks whether you have the reserves to spend, and none of the trials looked at people who run cold, tired or feel bloated and cramped. The research mostly asks whether the average healthy person benefits, and its participants were mostly young men.

Western medicine and its studies present claims towards the generalized, average person. It is universal and convenient. Chinese Medicine, while much more tedious, looks at each person as a unique system. There is no doubt that cold showers increase alertness, which may convince a tired body that the shower is good for it. It is like fanning a fire: you will certainly stimulate the flame, but the question is whether your fire can sustain it or be wiped out.

If you run warm, digest well, have strong circulation and show no chronic signs of cold, cold exposure may genuinely benefit you. A practitioner might call that robust yang, and you might call it good discipline. If you are always cold, tired after meals, bloated, prone to menstrual cramps and clots, and slow to warm up in the morning, a cold shower may not be building anything and may be depleting what you have. A practitioner might suggest warming instead, with ginger tea, a foot soak before bed, moxa or cooked, warming foods. Not because you are weak-minded, but because your fire needs tending, not testing. Temperature is a tool, not a test of character, and what strengthens one body may quietly drain another.

Also, TCM strongly pushes for warmth in days leading up to and throughout the menstrual cycle to promote blood flow and decrease menstrual cramps. This includes prioritizing warm baths or showers and hot foot soaks and not cold showers.

Next time you see a social media post about a morning cold shower or a cold plunge after the gym, ask yourself what your body actually needs right now: to be challenged, or to be warmed?
